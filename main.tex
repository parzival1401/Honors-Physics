\title{Electro-Optic and Galvanometer Characterization for High-Speed Beam Scanning in
MINFLUX Microscopy}
\author{Fernando Delgado}
\date{November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
MINFLUX locates a single fluorescent molecule by probing it with the dark center of a donut-shaped
excitation beam. The beam is stepped between a few positions around the molecule within every
cycle of about 100~\textmu s and re-centered on the molecule as it moves, so the precision of the
method rests on the hardware that moves the beam. This work characterizes that hardware for a
MINFLUX tracking instrument under construction: a pair of electro-optic deflectors (EODs) that step
the pattern, the donut they move, and a pair of galvanometer scanners that re-center it. The EODs
deflect linearly across $\pm200$~V, by 2.95 and 3.12~mrad/kV with the input polarization
perpendicular to the deflection axis and three and a half times less with it parallel.
They begin to move about half a microsecond after the drive and settle within 25~\textmu s, the
dwell of one pattern position, but arrive in two stages, reaching 80 to 94\% of a step within the
first 5~\textmu s. A vortex phase plate of charge one forms a donut whose central intensity is at
most 3 to 5\% of the ring peak, the limit the camera can resolve, and whose shape changes by about
one percent
under the full deflection of either axis; the same measurement shows the two deflection axes
$14^\circ$ from perpendicular. The galvanometers deflect the beam by $2.03^\circ$ and $1.44^\circ$
per volt, the lower value set by the fold geometry of the second mirror, return to within a few
microradians, and complete a move 0.4 to 0.45~ms after the command. Together these results give
the calibration and timing the control system needs, and they identify what remains: the depth of
the null with a better detector, the donut under two-axis scanning, and the transfer of every
scale to the sample plane.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{MINFLUX concept}
\label{sec:minflux}

A single fluorescent molecule seen through a microscope does not appear as a point. Diffraction
spreads its light into a spot a few hundred nanometers across, and the conventional way to locate
the molecule is to record that spot on a camera and find its center. The center can be found far
more finely than the spot is wide, but only by collecting many photons: the precision improves as
the width of the spot divided by the square root of the number of photons detected
\cite{oberLocalizationAccuracySinglemolecule2004,smithFastSinglemoleculeLocalization2010}. Every photon
carries only a little information, because each one could have landed anywhere in a spot hundreds
of nanometers wide. Reaching a few nanometers therefore takes thousands of photons, and a
fluorophore emits them slowly enough that camera-based tracking samples a molecule only every ten
milliseconds or so, at precisions above ten nanometers
\cite{eilersMINFLUXMonitorsRapid2018}. For tracking membrane proteins the frame time is
typically 10 to 30~ms \cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024}.

MINFLUX, for minimal photon fluxes, reverses where the information comes from
\cite{balzarottiNanometerResolutionImaging2017}. Instead of reading the position from the shape of
the emitted light, it reads it from the excitation light, which the experimenter controls. The
molecule is illuminated with a beam shaped as a donut: a ring of light around a central point
where the intensity falls to zero. A fluorophore emits at a rate proportional to the excitation
intensity it sees, so a molecule sitting exactly at the zero stays dark, and its emission rises
steeply as it moves away from it. The zero is placed in turn at a small set of known positions
around the molecule's estimated location, commonly three on a circle of diameter $L$ together
with one at the center, and the photons collected at each position are counted. The molecule is
closest to whichever position returns the fewest photons, and the ratios between the counts fix
where it lies between them. Combining those counts with the known exposure positions gives the
position estimate.

The gain comes from where the molecule sits on the excitation profile. Near the zero the
intensity changes fastest relative to its own value, so a small displacement produces a large
relative change in the counts. Each detected photon therefore says much more about position than a
camera photon does. In the first demonstration this required 22 times fewer photons than centroid
localization, reached about 1~nm precision, and increased the temporal resolution of single-molecule
tracking a hundredfold \cite{balzarottiNanometerResolutionImaging2017}. The precision is no longer
set by the width of a diffraction-limited spot but by the size of the probing pattern: it scales as
$L/\sqrt{N}$ for $N$ detected photons, with a prefactor set by the geometry of the pattern, so shrinking the pattern improves the precision at a fixed
photon budget. Since then MINFLUX has been extended to three dimensions and multiple colors in
cells \cite{gwoschMINFLUXNanoscopyDelivers2020,papeMulticolor3DMINFLUX2020}. It has also been
implemented on a standard microscope stand \cite{schmidtMINFLUXNanometerscale3D2021} and used to
follow molecular motion on sub-millisecond timescales. Fluorophores have been localized to about
2~nm in 400~\textmu s \cite{eilersMINFLUXMonitorsRapid2018}, and to better than 20~nm within about
100~\textmu s \cite{schmidtMINFLUXNanometerscale3D2021}. The steps of motor proteins have been
resolved inside living cells \cite{deguchiDirectObservationMotor2023}.

The same dependence on the pattern is the method's main liability. Because every position is
computed relative to where the zero is believed to have been placed, anything that moves, distorts
or mislocates the pattern biases the result, and that bias does not average away with more photons.
Simulations of fixed absorption dipoles and optical aberrations find systematic errors of up to
about a quarter of the pattern diameter \cite{stallingaAbsorptionDipoleEffects2026}. The quality of
the donut's zero has become a subject of its own \cite{lopezOptimizationCharacterizationToroidal2023},
and the sample must be held still to sub-nanometer precision
\cite{lopezOpensourceSubNanometerStabilization2025}.

\paragraph{MINFLUX in this project.} The project this work belongs to aims to measure how membrane
receptors pair up into dimers in living cells, and how long those pairs last. The receptors studied
are the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and its relative RON. Simulations predict fast
binding and unbinding events that video-rate tracking cannot capture
\cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024}. The proposal therefore calls for a MINFLUX system
that tracks single proteins and protein pairs at kHz rates, and combines it with a mode sorter that
measures the separation between the two members of a pair. Its worked example sets the scale of the
instrument. A scan cycle of 100~\textmu s, a pattern 30~nm across and 42 detected photons per cycle
localize a molecule with a precision of 1.7~nm at a rate of 10~kHz. The stated target is a
centroid precision below 2~nm from single fluorophores at 10~kHz
\cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024}.

\paragraph{Why two kinds of beam steering.} Tracking asks two different things of the optics that
move the excitation, and the proposal assigns them to two different devices
\cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024}. The first is to step the donut between the
positions of the pattern within every cycle. With four exposures in 100~\textmu s each position is
held for about 25~\textmu s, so a move between positions must take a small fraction of that. The
positions are only tens of nanometers apart in the sample, but they must be placed exactly,
because they are the reference against which the molecule is located. In the proposal this task
belongs to two electro-optic deflectors (EODs), one for each transverse axis, which translate the beam
``at MHz speed and high precision''. An EOD has no moving part, so it can
switch at electronic rates, but its range is small (Section~\ref{sec:eod}). A calibration error in
it scales or distorts the pattern itself, and so biases every localization in the way described
above.

The second task is to follow the molecule. A receptor diffusing in the membrane does not stay
inside a 30~nm pattern. The diffusion coefficients measured for EGFR, about 0.25 to
0.37~\textmu m$^2$/s \cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024}, carry it of order a
micrometer in a second, far beyond what the EODs can reach. The proposal therefore
re-centers the whole pattern on each new position estimate through a feedback loop driving a
two-axis galvanometer mirror system. Here range matters more than speed. A galvanometer moves a
mirror and so cannot jump within microseconds, but it can steer across the whole field, and it only
needs to keep pace with the molecule rather than with the scan cycle. MINFLUX built on a standard
microscope stand divides the work the same way, synchronizing electro-optic and galvanometric beam
steering
\cite{schmidtMINFLUXNanometerscale3D2021}. The EODs make the measurement and the galvanometer
keeps it centered on the molecule. The tracking precision depends on how well each is known, which
is why both are characterized in this work: the EOD pair in Section~\ref{sec:eod} and the
galvanometer in Section~\ref{sec:galvo}.
\subsection{Requirements and goals of this work}
\label{sec:goals}

The worked example of the proposal translates into requirements on each part of the beam-steering
hardware. The pattern positions are the reference for every localization, so they must be known
more finely than the precision sought. With a target below 2~nm and a pattern 30~nm across, a
scale error of one percent already moves the positions by 0.3~nm. The EODs must therefore be
calibrated to about a percent and must hold their positions to a fraction of a nanometer over a
measurement. Each position is held for about 25~\textmu s, and photons counted while the beam is
still moving are assigned to the wrong position, so a step must be complete within a small
fraction of that dwell. The zero must be dark, since light at the center of the donut is
indistinguishable from a molecule sitting off it, and it must stay dark and keep its shape wherever
the EODs place it. The re-centering must keep pace with the molecule. At the diffusion
coefficient of EGFR, a molecule wanders 15~nm, half the pattern, in about 0.2~ms, which sets the
scale against which the delay of the galvanometers has to be judged.

The proposal for this thesis set three goals against those requirements, all aimed at the
EODs:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item to characterize the deflection range and behavior of the EODs, establishing the
        parameters for precise high-speed scanning (Section~\ref{sec:eod});
    \item to implement the hardware control interface, a National Instruments data acquisition card
        driving the EODs through HVA200 high-voltage amplifiers (Section~\ref{sec:eod},
        Setup);
    \item to evaluate how deflection affects the donut, its shape and the integrity of its central
        null, under two-axis scanning (Section~\ref{sec:donut}).
\end{enumerate}
The galvanometer pair was added to that plan, because the re-centering it performs is the other half
of the steering and is calibrated with the same bench and the same methods
(Section~\ref{sec:galvo}). Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} returns to these goals and collects the
operating parameters the measurements establish.
\section{Electro-optic deflectors}
\label{sec:eod}
\subsection{Physical concept}

An electro-optic deflector (EOD) steers a laser beam without moving anything. It is a transparent
crystal with electrodes on its faces; applying a voltage changes the crystal's refractive index,
and that change alone is enough to turn the beam. This subsection sets out why that works, and
why it is worth the trouble it costs.

Light travels more slowly where the refractive index is higher. A beam of finite width can be
pictured as carrying a wavefront, the surface joining the points that are in step with one
another, and the beam travels in whatever direction that surface faces. Delay one side of the
beam more than the other and the surface tilts; the beam then leaves in a new direction.
Steering a beam therefore needs nothing more than a refractive index that differs across the
beam's width, and the tilt grows with both the size of that difference and the length of crystal
over which it acts.

The applied field is what produces the difference. In lithium tantalate the index shift is
proportional to the field, so doubling the voltage doubles the deflection and reversing the
voltage reverses it. A uniform field across a uniform slab, however, shifts the index everywhere
by the same amount: the whole wavefront is delayed, nothing tilts, and the device is a phase
modulator rather than an EOD. The index change has to vary across the aperture, and an
EOD is built so that it does, by dividing the crystal into sections whose orientation is
opposed, so that a single applied voltage raises the index on one side of the beam while lowering
it on the other.

The mechanism is the linear electro-optic, or Pockels, effect. A field $E$ changes the index seen
by light polarized along a given crystal axis by
\begin{equation}
    \Delta n = \tfrac{1}{2}\, n^3 r E ,
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the unperturbed index and $r$ the electro-optic coefficient that couples the field to
that polarization. With a voltage $V$ across an electrode gap $d$, the field is $V/d$, and opposed
sections turn the index change into a difference of $2\Delta n$ across a beam aperture $w$. Over
a crystal of length $L$ the wavefront then tilts by
\begin{equation}
    \theta \approx \frac{n^3 r L}{d\, w}\, V ,
\end{equation}
up to a factor of order unity set by the shape of the sections. Three properties of the device
follow directly. The deflection is proportional to the voltage. A long, thin crystal deflects
more per volt. And only the coefficient $r$ for the polarization actually present enters, so the
sensitivity depends on how the input light is polarized.

What makes this convenient is that nothing has to be accelerated. A galvanometer steers by
turning a mirror, and how quickly it can move and settle is set by the inertia of that mirror and
by the servo holding it in place. An EOD has no moving part at all, so its
speed is set instead by how fast charge can be pushed onto the electrodes, which is a question of
the crystal's capacitance and the amplifier driving it. The limit moves from mechanics to
electronics, and electronics is much the faster of the two. Two consequences follow. There is no
mechanical ringing to wait out, and no wear or vibration to manage. And because there is no
mass to accelerate, the time to move between two positions scarcely depends on how far apart they
are: positions can be addressed in any order at the same cost, where a mirror has to travel. The
drive electronics still take time to charge the crystal, and how long is measured below.
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-concept} sets the two ways of steering side by side.

That last property is why the technique suits this instrument. MINFLUX does not raster a beam
across a field; it parks the excitation at a small number of known positions and compares the
photon counts collected at each. Time spent moving and settling is time not spent collecting
photons, and the positions are revisited in rapid alternation rather than swept in sequence, so a
EOD that jumps between them at electronic speed is worth a great deal more than one that
covers a wide angle slowly. The EOD is also the ruler of the measurement. The position of an
emitter is computed from the photon counts and from where the pattern is believed to have been
placed, so any error in the conversion from drive voltage to beam position enters the
localization directly rather than averaging away. In this project the EOD pair therefore sets
both how fast the pattern can move and how well its positions are known.

The price is paid in three places. The angles available are small, milliradians rather than the
tens of degrees a mirror gives: at the 3~mrad/kV quoted for these units, the full $\pm200$~V of
the amplifier gives about $\pm0.6$~mrad, so the deflection has to be magnified and relayed by the
optics that follow. The drive voltages are high, which is why each crystal is fed from a high-voltage
amplifier rather than directly from a control card. And the response is referred to the crystal's
own axes, so how much deflection a given voltage produces depends on how the input polarization
is oriented with respect to those axes. In lithium tantalate the strongest coefficient, $r_{33}$,
acts on light polarized along the crystal's optic axis, while light polarized at right angles to
it sees $r_{13}$, about a quarter as large
\cite{yarivPhotonicsOpticalElectronics2007,cassonElectroopticCoefficientsLithium2004};
polarization therefore becomes part of the calibration rather than
a detail of the alignment, and is why a half-wave plate appears between the two EODs in the
arrangements described later.

It is worth naming the alternative, because it behaves quite differently. A second family of
EODs, built on potassium tantalate niobate, KTa$_{1-x}$Nb$_x$O$_3$ (KTN), reaches far
larger angles, 250~mrad from a few millimeters of crystal
\cite{nakamuraSpacechargecontrolledElectroopticEffect2008}, and does so by a different route: the
index gradient comes from electrons injected into the crystal, whose accumulated charge shapes
the internal field \cite{nakamuraSpacechargecontrolledElectroopticEffect2008,bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012}.
Those devices are fast and wide-ranging
\cite{bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012,miyazu400KHzBeam2010,yagiElectroopticKTNDevices2014},
but what they do depends on charge that has been put there and can move, so their behavior
drifts with operating conditions \cite{bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012}. The EODs used
here are not of that type. Their response comes from the crystal itself rather than from injected
charge, which is why the deflection is expected to follow the applied voltage proportionally, and
why that proportionality is something the measurements reported below can meaningfully test.
Against mechanical scanners, both solid-state families trade angular range for addressing rate,
exceeding conventional mirror scanners by up to an order of magnitude on the latter
\cite{romerElectroopticAcoustoopticLaser2014,heberleElectroopticAcoustoopticLaser2016}.

These EODs were chosen for this instrument for speed. Of the ways to steer the excitation,
an EOD is the one that can switch the pattern between its positions at
electronic rather than mechanical rates, and the MINFLUX cycle is built on exactly that switching;
the small angle and high voltage are the costs accepted for it. Concretely, the instrument carries
two Leysop \texttt{ED2-2T-LT-AR561-642} EODs, one per transverse axis, with a 2~mm clear
aperture in a 60~mm housing and an antireflection coating covering both the 561 and 642~nm
excitation lines. Each has two electrode inputs on MHV high-voltage coaxial connectors; one is
driven by a Thorlabs HVA200 high-voltage amplifier (gain $-20$, $\pm200$~V) and the other held at
ground. In the instrument each amplifier is to be fed from the instrument's field-programmable gate
array (FPGA), the controller that times the MINFLUX cycle, through a fast digital-to-analog converter and an op-amp preamplifier stage; on the bench
it is fed from a data acquisition card through a voltage follower, as described under Setup.
The vendor supplies no datasheet for these semi-custom units; the only performance figure given
in writing is a quoted minimum deflection sensitivity of ${>}3$~mrad/kV, and the deflection pivot
is stated to lie at the center of the crystal, which sits at the center of the housing. Everything else used by the control system is
established by the measurements reported below.

The two EODs are referred to throughout by serial number. EOD34 is the first in the beam
path, steers the $x$ axis, and is driven by amplifier HVA1; EOD35 follows it, steers $y$, and is
driven by HVA2. Every result below is attributed to one or the other by these names.

\subsection{Characterization}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The EOD pair is what moves the excitation pattern between the positions of a MINFLUX cycle, and the
control system commands that motion in volts while the experiment is defined in nanometers at the
sample. Everything in this subsection exists to establish that conversion and to bound how far it
can be trusted. The need is sharper here than for a catalog component, because these are
semi-custom units for which the manufacturer supplies no datasheet: the single performance figure
available in writing is a quoted minimum sensitivity of ${>}3$~mrad/kV. A quoted minimum is not a
calibration. It cannot say whether EOD34 and EOD35 match, whether the response is linear across the
drive range, how the sensitivity depends on input polarization, or how quickly the deflection
settles after a commanded step, and each of those enters the position of the excitation pattern
directly.

Linearity is the first of these. The scan pattern is only faithful if the mapping from drive voltage
to deflection angle is proportional across the range actually used, and a departure from
proportionality displaces the pattern relative to where the control system believes it to be. The
consequences are not hypothetical: in high-resolution force-clamp trapping, the nonlinear response
of acousto-optic deflectors introduced experimental artifacts that were removed by substituting
electro-optic deflectors \cite{woodyElectroopticDeflectorsDeliver2018}. The same logic applies
within a single EOD across its own drive range.

Polarization is the second, and it is specific to the linear electro-optic mechanism described
above. Because the response is referred to the crystal axes, the sensitivity obtained depends on the
orientation of the input polarization, and the rotational alignment of each EOD to its crystal
axis is therefore a calibration parameter rather than a one-time alignment step. This is the reason
the input polarization appears among the quantities varied below.

Timing is the third. The photon economy of MINFLUX is what opens the microsecond regime in which
molecular jumps and conformational changes become observable
\cite{eilersMINFLUXMonitorsRapid2018}, and reaching that regime on a conventional microscope stand
has depended specifically on synchronized electro-optical and galvanometric beam steering together
with sub-nanometer sample stabilization \cite{schmidtMINFLUXNanometerscale3D2021}. The requirement
this places on the EOD is that the step settling of the whole drive chain be small compared
with the dwell at each position of the pattern, so that the excitation is stationary during every
sub-exposure. Whether that holds is a property of the drive electronics as much as of the crystal. The camera-rate measurements reported below cannot reach that timescale; they establish
whether a held deflection stays put over seconds. The microsecond step response is measured
separately, with a fast position detector in place of the camera.

Beam quality is the fourth, and it is a question about what the EOD hands downstream. The EODs
sit ahead of the element that shapes the excitation pattern, so a Gaussian beam that leaves them
distorted, clipped by the 2~mm aperture, or displaced as a function of deflection angle compromises
the pattern formed later in the path. Profile deformation that depends on the deflection midpoint
and amplitude has been documented for electro-optic scanning, albeit in a different crystal system
and with femtosecond pulses, where fluence was the dominant variable
\cite{bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012}; the fluence dependence is not expected to transfer to the
present continuous-wave regime, but the deflection-angle dependence is exactly the behavior the
beam-quality measurements below are designed to detect.

Finally, the geometry through which an angle is inferred has to be measured rather than assumed.
Deflection angle is not observed directly but reconstructed from a displacement on a camera and the
distance from the crystal to that camera, so an error in the assumed lever arm propagates in full
into the quoted sensitivity. The measurements reported here therefore refer the lever arm explicitly
to the deflection pivot at the center of the crystal rather than to the face of its housing.

\subsubsection{Setup}

Every measurement below except the step response records the beam directly on the camera sensor,
and each reduces the recorded frames by the procedures set out under Methods.

\paragraph{Drive electronics.}
Every measurement that drives the EODs uses the circuit of
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-drive-circuit}. The two analog outputs of an NI USB-6001 card, AO0 and AO1,
issue the command voltages for EOD34 and EOD35. Each passes through an LF411 operational amplifier
wired as a unity-gain voltage follower to the input of its HVA200, HVA1 for EOD34 and HVA2 for
EOD35, and the amplifier's output drives the crystal. The amplifier inverts, with a gain of $-20$.
The card reads back each command on AI1 and AI3, and each amplifier's monitor output, one
twentieth of its high-voltage output, on AI0 and AI2, so both ends of the chain are recorded at
every step.

The followers are there because without them the amplifiers could not be driven across their
full range. In the first configuration, the card's outputs were connected directly to the
amplifier inputs, and a ramp of the command from $-10$ to $+10$~V reached neither end: both the
command read back at the card and the amplifier monitor followed the setpoint only up to about
7~to~8~V of either sign and then stayed flat, which limited the drive to the crystals to roughly
$\pm135$ to $\pm160$~V instead of $\pm200$~V. The cause is an impedance mismatch. The HVA200 has
an input impedance of only 1~k$\Omega$, so a 10~V command must supply 10~mA, while the USB-6001's
analog outputs are rated for $\pm5$~mA. Measured at the point of saturation, the two outputs
delivered 6.70 and 7.68~mA into amplifier inputs of 1.002 and 1.045~k$\Omega$. Those products,
6.71 and 8.03~V, are the voltages at which the two ramps flattened, so the outputs had reached the
limit of the current they could supply. A voltage follower removes the mismatch. Its input draws
almost no current from the card, and its output supplies the amplifier's 10~mA from its own
$\pm15$~V supply, passing the command through unchanged. With the followers in place, the command
and the monitor follow the setpoint linearly across the full $\pm10$~V, and the amplifiers reach
$\pm200$~V with their gain of $-20$. The external supply at first added high-frequency noise to the
command. Decoupling capacitors from each supply rail to ground removed it. The circuit was built
before any of the driven measurements reported below.

This card is the control path for calibration and for every measurement here in which the drive is
held or swept slowly. It is not the path for switching the pattern. The USB-6001 updates its
outputs at most 5000 times per second, far too slowly to step between four positions in
100~\textmu s, so the step response below is driven by a function generator instead, and in the
instrument the fast command comes from the FPGA through a high-speed digital-to-analog converter,
as described above.

\paragraph{Deflection sensitivity and polarization.}
On the same bench, EOD34 and EOD35 are each swept across roughly $\pm200$~V of amplifier output
while the camera records the beam center at every step. The sweep is repeated at three input
polarizations, set by a linear polarizer ahead of the crystal: parallel to the deflection axis,
perpendicular to it, and with the polarizer removed. The crystal-to-sensor distance, which
converts displacement into angle, is measured on the bench as 190~mm from the face of the
EOD housing; the analysis refers it to the deflection pivot at the center of the crystal,
30~mm further back, for a lever arm of 220~mm.

\paragraph{Pointing stability.}
Stability is measured on a separate bench with a longer lever arm, 350~mm from the housing face
to the sensor and so 380~mm from the deflection pivot. EOD34 and EOD35 are each held in turn at
nine command voltages from $-10$ to $+10$~V in steps of 2.5~V, and the camera records 40 frames
at each held voltage, about 0.9~s apart, so that every hold lasts roughly 36~s. A baseline run
records the beam for 673~s, about 0.67~s per frame, with the EOD drive inactive: the
amplifier monitor stays within 0.5~V and the beam does not move with it, so that run
measures the pointing stability of the laser and the bench alone, against which the EODs
are compared. Neither run resolves anything faster than the camera frame interval.

\paragraph{Step response.}
How quickly the beam follows a change of drive is measured without the camera, with the command
supplied by a Siglent SDG810 function generator. The deflected beam
falls on a Thorlabs PDQ80A quadrant photodiode, whose difference output is proportional to the
position of the beam on the detector for small displacements, with a specified bandwidth of
150~kHz. Each EOD in turn is driven with a square wave at frequencies from 1 to 50~kHz, with
a command of about 4.4~V peak to peak and so of the order of 90~V at the amplifier output. A
two-channel oscilloscope records the monitor output of the HVA200 amplifier on one channel and the detector on
the other. Because the reference is the amplifier's own output, the timing measured is that of
the EOD and the detection, from the voltage reaching the crystal to the beam arriving at its
new position, and excludes the amplifier itself.

\subsubsection{Methods}

Every camera measurement in this section reduces a frame to a few numbers describing the beam,
so the reduction is set out once here and then referred to. All lengths are converted from pixels
using a sensor pitch of 5.2~\textmu m.

\paragraph{Preparing a frame.}
A background level is formed as the mean of the dimmest five percent of pixels in the frame and
subtracted uniformly, and the result is clipped at zero so that noise below background cannot
contribute negative weight. The beam center is then located by a thresholded centroid rather than
by the brightest pixel. The distinction is not cosmetic: because the center selects which row and
column become the measured profiles, and the peak of those profiles sets the half-maximum
reference, a single hot pixel taken as the center propagates into every width derived from that
frame.

\paragraph{Measuring a width.}
Profiles through the center are averaged over a band of eleven lines either side rather than read
from a single line. For a separable Gaussian this is exact: averaging symmetric rows returns the
same transverse profile scaled by a constant, so the width is preserved while the noise falls as
the square root of the number of lines. The frames here peak at only tens of counts, and on a
single line one noise dip is enough to terminate the width search early. The averaged profile is
then smoothed with a symmetric box filter three pixels either side, chosen because a zero-phase
filter cannot move the position of a crossing.

The width is taken as the contiguous run of the profile that lies above half of its maximum
\emph{and contains the center}, with both crossings linearly interpolated between the bracketing
samples. Searching instead for the first and last sample anywhere above half maximum, which is
the obvious implementation, is not equivalent: a second spot elsewhere in the line, or a single
bright pixel, stretches the reported width from one to the other.

Ellipticity is the ratio of the larger to the smaller of the two axis widths, so unity denotes a
circular beam.

\paragraph{Fitting a Gaussian.}
A circularly symmetric Gaussian on a constant pedestal,
\begin{equation}
    I(x,y) = C \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\left[(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2\right]}{\omega^2}\right) + b ,
\end{equation}
is fitted by Nelder--Mead least squares, to at most five thousand iterations, initialized from
the measured peak amplitude, the centroid, the background level, and a radius taken from the
profile width. The model carries no rotation angle and no separate widths along the two axes; it
is the ellipticity above, not the fit, that reports departure from circularity.

The fit runs on a square crop about the center whose half-side is two and a half times the
starting radius, floored at 250~pixels. Scaling the crop to the beam matters: at the expanded end
of this bench the spot is several hundred pixels across, and a crop that holds less than the core
drives the coefficient of determination down to a value that reads as ``not Gaussian'' when it
means only that the window was too small to contain the Gaussian. The reported $R^2$ is computed
against that crop.

\paragraph{Two radii, and why they are reported together.}
With the model written as above, $\omega$ is the radius at which the intensity falls to $1/e^2$
of its peak, and for that convention the full width at half maximum (FWHM) and the radius are
related by $\mathrm{FWHM} = \omega\sqrt{2\ln 2}$. A second radius is therefore obtained from the measured
width alone, as the mean of the two axis widths divided by $\sqrt{2\ln 2} \approx 1.1774$,
without reference to the fit.

For a true Gaussian the two must agree, which is the point of computing both. They separate when
the profile carries wings the model does not describe, so the gap between them is a measurement
of how far the beam departs from Gaussian rather than a disagreement between two estimates of the
same thing. The constant is convention-sensitive: the factor $2\sqrt{\ln 2}$ belongs to the
alternative definition $I \propto \exp(-r^2/\omega^2)$, and using it beside a $1/e^2$ fit
separates the two radii by a flat factor of $\sqrt{2}$ for purely notational reasons.

\paragraph{Calibrating deflection against drive.}
The high-voltage amplifier presents a monitor output scaled to one twentieth of its true output
and inverted; the drive applied to the crystal is recovered by negating that reading and
multiplying by twenty. Each sweep step records this monitor value together with the commanded
output and a frame.

Deflection is obtained from the displacement of the beam center across the sweep. With the
crystal-to-sensor distance $z$ as the lever arm, the angle follows from the displacement as
$\theta \simeq \Delta x / z$ in the small-angle limit, which at the milliradian angles here is
indistinguishable from the exact inverse tangent. The sensitivity is not read from the endpoints
but from an ordinary least-squares regression of beam center against recovered drive voltage
across the whole sweep, expressed in milliradians per kilovolt and compared against the
manufacturer's quoted minimum of 3~mrad/kV.

\paragraph{Comparing a frame against the control.}
Where a frame is compared with an undeflected reference, both are mean-subtracted and their
two-dimensional cross-correlation is computed by Fourier transform, normalized by the square root
of the product of their summed squares, so that a peak of unity denotes identical frames up to a
shift. The peak value measures how far the beam still resembles the reference, and the location
of that peak relative to zero lag gives the displacement between them.

\paragraph{Quantifying pointing stability.}
Within each held voltage the beam center of every frame is referred to the mean center of that
hold, so that the deflection itself is removed and only the wander about it remains. The spread
of that wander is summarized by its standard deviation and its peak-to-peak range, computed on
the radial displacement, and converted to angle through the lever arm as above. At 380~mm one
camera pixel corresponds to about 0.014~mrad.

\paragraph{Timing an edge.}
The oscilloscope stores 2500 points per channel at 8 bits, with a sample interval that ranges
from 2~\textmu s in the slowest captures to 0.1~\textmu s in the fastest. Each drive edge is
located at the time its trace crosses half its swing, interpolated between samples; the half-way
point is used rather than the customary tenth because, at the vertical scale recorded, one
quantization level is already about a tenth of the drive swing, and a threshold that low fires on
noise. The raw detector samples around every edge of a capture are aligned on those times and
averaged, without smoothing. The detector signal is then expressed as a fraction of the full
throw, the difference between the two levels at which the beam comes to rest, taken from the
captures whose half period is long enough, 40~\textmu s or more, for the beam to settle; the
detector gain is the same in every file, so the throw carries over to the faster captures where
the beam never settles. The delay is the time from the drive's half-swing crossing to the beam
reaching a tenth of its throw, and a delay shorter than one sample interval is reported as
unresolved. The approach is summarized in two ways: by the fraction of the throw reached 5, 10 and
25~\textmu s after the edge, and by a fit to the averaged steps from 10 to 50~kHz of a delayed
two-stage response,
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = 1 - a\,e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_f} - (1-a)\,e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau_s}, \qquad t > t_0,
\end{equation}
with a delay $t_0$, a fast stage of share $a$ and time constant $\tau_f$, and a slow stage of time
constant $\tau_s$. Rising and falling edges are treated separately, and captures found to be
byte-for-byte copies of another are discarded.

crystals and their mounting rather than design. The clearest result of this measurement is a
working range rather than a degradation curve. The

If the perpendicular setting puts the light along the optic axis and the parallel setting at
right angles to it, the two sensitivities stand in the ratio of the coefficients $r_{33}$ and

ratio at the low end of that range is expected, since any small misalignment of the polarizer
mixes some of the stronger response into the weaker setting.

The values with the polarizer removed behave differently, and the difference is instructive. The
laser is linearly polarized at its output, and the polarization-maintaining fiber that brings it to
the bench keeps it so, so with the polarizer removed each crystal still sees
linearly polarized light, but at whatever angle $\varphi$ to its optic axis the source and the
optics before it happen to set. The light then divides between the two axes, and the measured
sensitivity is the weighted mean
\begin{equation}
    S(\varphi) = S_\perp \cos^2\varphi + S_\parallel \sin^2\varphi ,
\end{equation}
which places the input about $40^\circ$ from the optic axis of EOD34 and about $23^\circ$ from
that of EOD35. That is why this condition is where the two EODs disagree most, by a third,
whereas at the two controlled settings they agree to within about six percent: the gap belongs to
how each crystal sits relative to the laser's polarization, not to the crystals.

Measured against the manufacturer's quoted minimum of 3~mrad/kV, the outcome depends on the
polarization first and on the EOD second. Only at the perpendicular setting do the
EODs approach the specification at all: EOD35 meets it, and EOD34 falls short by under two
percent, a margin comparable to the uncertainty in the lever arm discussed below. With the polarizer removed,
both fall below it, and at the parallel setting both fail by a factor of
about three and a half. A half-wave plate correctly set is therefore a precondition for the
EODs reaching their quoted performance, not an optimization applied on top of it.

Two consequences follow for the control system. The orientation of the input polarization has to
be fixed and recorded as part of the calibration, since it alone moves the volts-to-angle
conversion across that range. And even with the polarization controlled, EOD34 and EOD35 differ
by about six percent, so a scan pattern built on a single sensitivity for both axes would be
stretched along one of them; each EOD needs its own calibration.

Two cautions attach to these numbers. They are inversely proportional to the lever arm, taken
here as 220~mm: the 190~mm measured on the bench runs from the housing face to the sensor, and
the manufacturer places the deflection pivot at the center of the 60~mm housing, a further
30~mm back. Referring the angle to the housing face instead would overstate every sensitivity by
about sixteen percent and carry both EODs above the specification at the perpendicular
setting, so where that distance is measured from is not a detail. And the first recorded point
of every sweep, at the negative extreme of the drive, sits off the line established by the
remainder; it has the character of a settling transient and is excluded from the regressions.

\paragraph{Step response.}
The beam follows a step of drive after a short delay and in two stages,
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-delay}. It begins to move between 0.42 and 0.53~\textmu s after the amplifier
output crosses half its swing, measured to the point where the beam has covered a tenth of its
throw, for both EODs at every frequency from 10 to 50~kHz,
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-delay-vs-frequency}. Falling edges agree with rising ones to within
0.06~\textmu s, and the delay shows no trend with frequency beyond that scatter. A detector with a
150~kHz bandwidth would itself lag a step's tenth point by about 0.1~\textmu s, so at most that
much of the delay belongs to the detection; the rest, about 0.3 to 0.5~\textmu s, is the time for
the voltage at the amplifier output to begin moving the beam. At 1, 2 and, for EOD35, 5~kHz the
oscilloscope samples too coarsely to resolve a delay this short.

The approach to the new position is not a single exponential. Averaged over all the edges of a
capture, the step consists of a fast stage, which carries 41 percent of the throw for EOD34 with a
time constant of 0.32~\textmu s and 59 percent for EOD35 with 0.85~\textmu s, and a slow stage,
which carries the remainder with a time constant of 3.7~\textmu s for EOD34 and 2.8~\textmu s for
EOD35, Figure~\ref{fig:eod-delay-step-response}. In practical terms, 5~\textmu s after the edge
EOD34 has covered 80 to 82 percent of its throw and EOD35 91 to 94 percent; at 10~\textmu s the
figures are 96 to 97 and 98 to 100 percent; and by 25~\textmu s both have arrived within the noise,
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-delay-settled-fraction}. The averaged steps from 10 to 50~kHz lie on top of one
another, so the response is the same at every rate tested: at 50~kHz the half period of
10~\textmu s simply ends before the slow stage is complete, Figure~\ref{fig:eod-delay-trace-50khz},
and that truncation, not a faster response, is why a rise time read from each trace in isolation
appears to shorten as the frequency rises.

Neither stage can be laid entirely on the detector. The fast stage of EOD34, at 0.32~\textmu s, is
quicker than a single 150~kHz pole would allow, so the detector on this path responds faster than
its specified bandwidth; the fast time constants are therefore best read as upper bounds on the
time the EOD itself takes, which may respond faster still. The slow stage, at 3 to 4~\textmu s, is several times slower
than the detector has just been shown to be, and a single detector pole cannot in any case produce
two separate stages. It belongs to the drive or to the crystal. The amplifier monitor shows a clean
edge, 0.37 to 0.51~\textmu s from ten to ninety percent, but it need not report the voltage
across the crystal itself, and a slow charging of the crystal's capacitance through the cable and
the amplifier output might not appear on it. These data cannot separate that from a response of
the crystal itself.

For the MINFLUX cycle of four positions in 100~\textmu s, each held for about 25~\textmu s, the
EODs arrive within every dwell, but not at its start. During the first 5~\textmu s of a dwell
EOD34 is still up to a fifth of the step short of its target and EOD35 up to a tenth, so photons
counted then are counted at an intermediate position. The delay of half a microsecond is
negligible against the dwell and, being fixed, could be compensated in timing; the slow stage is
what matters.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}

The EODs pass the beam with its shape largely intact, provided they are aligned within
their working range. Translated across the beam, each reproduces the control to a correlation
near unity only over about 1.5~mm (EOD34) to 2~mm (EOD35) of stage travel, so lateral alignment of the crystal, not the
crystal itself, is the first limit on beam quality. Within that range, driving the EODs
across their full span leaves the beam within 14 percent of circular, and along the assembled
path the ellipticity stays below 1.2 at every station that can be measured. What the path does
cost is Gaussian character: the fit quality falls from 0.965 at the fiber to 0.90 through EOD34,
the first relay and EOD35, and the two independent radii disagree by a tenth or more throughout,
so the beam handed downstream carries wings that a Gaussian model does not describe.

The deflection is linear in drive across the full $\pm200$~V for both EODs, with no
curvature visible at either extreme, which is what the bulk electro-optic mechanism predicts. Its
magnitude, however, is set as much by polarization as by the device. Only with the input
perpendicular to the deflection axis do the EODs approach the quoted 3~mrad/kV: EOD35
meets it at 3.12, and EOD34 falls just short at 2.95, within the uncertainty of the lever arm.
Polarization along the wrong axis costs a factor of three and a half, close to the ratio of the
crystal's two electro-optic coefficients, and leaving it uncontrolled makes the sensitivity
depend on how the laser's polarization meets each crystal rather than on the device. With the polarization fixed, EOD34 and EOD35 still differ by about
six percent, so each needs its own calibration.

Held at a fixed voltage, EOD35 adds no measurable wander to that of the laser beneath it, about
0.001~mrad within a hold. EOD34
is equally steady over most of its range but drifts slowly when held at zero or at most negative
drives, by up to 0.03~mrad over half a minute. For MINFLUX acquisition this matters less as noise
than as a systematic offset: a pattern calibrated on EOD34 shortly after a large step would carry
the drift into its positions.

On the microsecond scale the EODs are fast enough for the MINFLUX cycle, but not
instantaneous. The beam begins to move about half a microsecond after the amplifier output, a
fixed delay that timing can absorb, and then arrives in two stages: a fast stage within about a
microsecond, carrying 41 percent of the step for EOD34 and 59 percent for EOD35, and the remainder
over a slower stage with a time constant of 3 to 4~\textmu s. Both
EODs are within the noise of their target by 25~\textmu s, the dwell of each position in a
100~\textmu s cycle of four, but in the first 5~\textmu s of a dwell EOD34 is up to a fifth of the
step short and EOD35 up to a tenth. The response is the same at every rate from 10 to 50~kHz, so it
is a fixed property of the chain rather than a limit that tightens with speed.

For operation, these results recommend fixing the input polarization perpendicular to each
deflection axis with the half-wave plate and recording it as part of the calibration; calibrating
EOD34 and EOD35 separately, each against a lever arm referred to the crystal center; centering each
crystal laterally within its working range; until the drift of EOD34 is understood, allowing
it to settle, or recalibrating, after each change of drive, particularly near zero and negative
drive; and either discarding the
photons of the first few microseconds of each dwell or shaping the drive step to cancel the slow
stage, which is possible precisely because the response does not change with rate.

Several questions remain open. The origin of the slow stage of the step response, in the
amplifier charging the crystal or in the crystal itself, could be settled by measuring the voltage
across the crystal with a high-voltage probe alongside the beam; and the fast stage is resolved
only as an upper bound, because the detector on this path is at the limit of its speed. Whether EOD34 meets
its specification turns on the lever arm: its shortfall corresponds to about 4~mm in 220. The first relay cannot be
separated from EOD35, because station P3 is inaccessible; the station data rest on one frame
each; and the final station, after the beam expander, is too dim to interpret. The origin
of the non-Gaussian wings, and of the slow drift in EOD34 alone, has yet to be identified.
\section{Donut beam}
\label{sec:donut}
\subsection{Physical concept}

The excitation beam of MINFLUX is a ring of light around a dark center, and the dark center is the
part that matters: it is the reference point against which every emitter is located. A beam with a
true zero on its axis cannot be made by blocking the center of an ordinary beam, because
diffraction refills any obscured region as the beam propagates. It is made instead by giving the
beam a twisted wavefront. If the field carries a phase that winds around the axis,
\begin{equation}
    E(r,\phi) = A(r)\,e^{i\ell\phi},
\end{equation}
with $\ell$ an integer, then on the axis itself the phase takes every value at once and the field
has no single value to take. The only consistent amplitude there is zero. The beam therefore has a
dark line along its axis that is enforced by the geometry of the phase rather than by any balance
of amplitudes, so it persists as the beam propagates and is focused and does not depend on the
size of the beam. The integer $\ell$ is the topological charge, and such beams carry an orbital
angular momentum of $\ell\hbar$ per photon \cite{allenOrbitalAngularMomentum1992}.

The simplest beams of this kind are the Laguerre--Gaussian modes. With no radial nodes their
intensity is
\begin{equation}
    I(r) = I_0 \left(\frac{2r^2}{w^2}\right)^{|\ell|} e^{-2r^2/w^2},
    \label{eq:lg-intensity}
\end{equation}
a single bright ring of radius $r_\mathrm{peak} = w\sqrt{|\ell|/2}$ around the zero
\cite{allenOrbitalAngularMomentum1992}. Near the axis the intensity rises as $r^{2|\ell|}$. For
charge one that rise is quadratic, which is the steep, well-defined dependence on position that
MINFLUX exploits \cite{balzarottiNanometerResolutionImaging2017}; a charge-two beam would rise as
$r^4$, leaving a broader, flatter dark region that says less about where an emitter sits. The
instrument uses charge one.

The twist is applied by a spiral phase plate, a transparent plate whose thickness rises steadily
with angle around its center, by one wavelength's worth of optical path over a full turn
\cite{beijersbergenHelicalwavefrontLaserBeams1994}. A beam crossing it acquires the phase
$e^{i\phi}$ without any change to its intensity, so immediately after the plate the beam is still
Gaussian in brightness; the dark core opens only as it propagates, and it is fully formed in the
focal plane of a lens. What forms there is not a single Laguerre--Gaussian mode. A Gaussian beam
given a vortex phase is a superposition of charge-one modes with several radial orders, a
hypergeometric-Gaussian beam \cite{karimiHypergeometricGaussianModes2007}, which shares the zero on
the axis and the quadratic rise around it but carries weaker outer wings than
Equation~\ref{eq:lg-intensity} predicts. A fit to the single mode is therefore expected to describe
the core and the ring better than the tails, and that is how it is used below.

Two properties of the plate set limits on the null. The plate is cut for 633~nm and is used here
at 642~nm, so its phase step falls short of a full turn by about one and a half percent. A winding
of $\alpha$ turns, with $\alpha$ not an integer, is a mixture of integer charges in which charge $m$
carries the fraction $\mathrm{sinc}^2[\pi(\alpha-m)]$ of the power. For $\alpha \approx 0.986$ the
uncharged part, which is bright on the axis, carries about $2\times10^{-4}$ of the power, enough to
lift the floor of the null slightly but far below what the camera used here can resolve. The second
is centering. Moving the plate off the beam axis does not remove the zero, which the topology
guarantees, but displaces it from the center of the beam and makes the ring brighter on one side
than the other; an uneven ring is the signature of a decentered plate.

Polarization adds one more condition, but only at high numerical aperture. When the beam is
focused tightly by a microscope objective, part of the field turns to point along the axis, and that
component can fill the zero unless the beam is circularly polarized with the handedness opposite to
the twist of its phase \cite{lopezOptimizationCharacterizationToroidal2023}. This is why a
quarter-wave plate sets the beam to circular polarization before it is focused, both on the bench
below and in the instrument \cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024}; since the vortex
plate imposes the same phase on any polarization, the waveplate may stand on either side of it.
The bench measurements
focus the donut with a single lens of low numerical aperture, where the field remains transverse
and the polarization should not affect the null, so they test the vortex itself; the handedness
condition has to be verified at the focus of the objective.

\subsection{Characterization}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

Every MINFLUX position is referred to the zero of the excitation pattern, so, as the Introduction
set out, a distorted or shallow zero biases the measurement rather than merely adding noise, and
that bias does not average away with more photons \cite{stallingaAbsorptionDipoleEffects2026}.
Three properties of the donut therefore decide whether it can serve. The first is how dark the
center is relative to the ring: light at the zero cannot be told apart from a molecule sitting off
it. The second is how symmetric the ring is around the zero, since an uneven ring shifts the
effective center of the pattern. The third is whether both survive being moved. The pattern is
stepped between its positions by the EODs characterized in Section~\ref{sec:eod}, and a null
that degrades with deflection would make the localization bias depend on the position in the
pattern. The measurements below address each property in turn.

\subsubsection{Setup}

The donut is formed on the relayed EOD bench of Section~\ref{sec:eod}, extended by a lens
train and the vortex plate, Figure~\ref{fig:donut-setup-creation}. The 642~nm source is folded by
two mirrors into EOD34, whose deflection plane lenses L1 and L2 relay onto EOD35, with a half-wave
plate between them setting the input polarization of the second crystal. Every lens has a focal
length of 100~mm except L5, of 13.5~mm. Two further relays follow EOD35, each a pair of lenses
separated by the sum of their focal lengths: L3 and L4 image the deflection plane of EOD35 at unit
magnification onto an intermediate plane one focal length beyond L4, and L5 and L6 image that plane
in turn onto a plane one focal length beyond L6, conjugate to both EODs. In the naming of the
stations of Section~\ref{sec:eod}, L1 and L2 form the first relay, L3 and L4 the second, and L5 and
L6 the beam expander. Its magnification of $100/13.5\approx7.4$ should take the beam of about
1~mm that passes the EODs to about 7~mm, inside the 5 to 10~mm window recommended for the
plate, and reduce every deflection angle by the same factor; the beam size at the plate was not
measured. The vortex phase plate, a Vortex Photonics \texttt{V-633-10-1} of charge one, sits in
that conjugate plane. A deflection therefore tilts the beam as it crosses the plate without moving
it across the plate's center, which is the arrangement the instrument uses to steer the donut
without degrading it. Mirror M3 then folds the beam through a quarter-wave plate, which sets it to
circular polarization, and into lens L7. The camera lies at the focal plane of L7, where the dark
core is fully formed,
and because a lens maps angle to position in its focal plane, a deflection appears as a
displacement of the donut on the sensor, recorded directly with 5.2~\textmu m pixels.

Three measurements were made in a single session. The first records the donut at rest, both
EODs at 0~V, as single frames: a characterization frame and a second frame taken after the
alignment had been adjusted to darken the center. The second compares the donut with and without a
polarizer ahead of the quarter-wave plate, and then with the waveplates set for the darkest center.
The third sweeps each EOD in turn across its full range while the donut is recorded, to test
whether the null survives deflection. A control frame is taken with both EODs at 0~V, and then
EOD34 is swept from $+10$ to $-10$~V of command voltage, the full $\pm200$~V at the amplifier, in
200 steps of about 0.1~V and roughly half a second each, with EOD35 held at 0~V; EOD35 is then
swept the same way with EOD34 held at 0~V.

\subsubsection{Methods}

Every frame is recorded by a Thorlabs DCx camera in 8-bit monochrome mode with a 10~ms exposure, so
intensities are in camera units (ADU) from 0 to 255; the gain that converts them to photons is not
known. Each frame is reduced to a small set of numbers describing the donut. The background is the
mean level in an annulus five to eight ring radii from the center, well clear of the beam.

The shape of the donut is compared with Equation~\ref{eq:lg-intensity} by a two-dimensional
least-squares fit over a square of $81\times81$ pixels around the beam, with the center, the
amplitude, the waist $w$ and a constant background all free. The fit is made for charge one, for
charge two, and for a Gaussian, on the same pixels, and the goodness of fit $R^2$ of each is
reported; a donut that fits a Gaussian better than a vortex has failed to form. The fitted center
also serves as the center of the ring for every quantity below. From the azimuthally averaged radial
profile about that center, in one-pixel bins, come two model-free sizes: the radius of peak
intensity and the outer radius at which the intensity falls to $1/e^2$ of that peak. The matching
inner radius lies within a few pixels of the null, where it cannot be resolved, and is not used.

The depth of the null is reported as the extinction ratio, the background-subtracted intensity at
the null divided by that at the ring peak; lower is better. The ring is often brighter on one side,
which moves its darkest point away from the ring center, so the null is located as the darkest
$3\times3$-pixel spot within half a ring radius of the center and its intensity taken as the mean of
the nine pixels there. The ring peak is the maximum of the background-subtracted azimuthal average.
Treating each unit as a count gives the uncertainty of the null. Where the net null is less than
twice its uncertainty it is not resolved, and the result is reported as an upper bound, the larger
of the net null plus twice its uncertainty and one unit, divided by the ring peak. Two floors frame
these numbers. One unit divided by the ring peak, between 0.02 and 0.08 here, is the smallest
extinction ratio the camera can register. And because a nine-pixel window has finite size, even a
perfect charge-one ring sampled this way would read about 0.013.

The symmetry of the ring is measured on a circle at the radius of peak intensity, sampling the
intensity around the full turn. The spread of that azimuthal profile, as a coefficient of variation,
measures how uneven the ring is, and its Fourier components separate the causes. Writing the profile
as $I(\theta)\approx I_0\left[1+\sum_k m_k\cos(k\theta-\theta_k)\right]$, the first component,
$m_1$, describes a ring brighter on one side, the signature of a decentered plate or a tilted beam;
the second, $m_2$, an elliptical ring, as from astigmatism; and the fourth, $m_4$, a fourfold
distortion.

For the deflection sweep, the center of every frame is located by the same fit and its displacement
taken relative to the control frame, and straight lines are fitted to the displacement against
command voltage along both camera axes. The extinction ratio is recomputed for every frame. The
shape of each frame is compared with the control by the normalized cross-correlation of a
101-pixel square cropped about each frame's own center, which is unity for an identical pattern;
because each crop follows its donut, this measures a change of shape rather than the displacement.
The first frame of the EOD34 sweep, recorded at $+10$~V before the beam had moved, carries the same
settling transient as the deflection sweeps of Section~\ref{sec:eod} and is excluded; the first
frame of the EOD35 sweep agrees with those that follow it and is kept.

\paragraph{The donut under deflection.}
The donut follows the EODs linearly and keeps its shape. Driving EOD34 moves it along the
camera $x$ axis at $4.82$~\textmu m/V of command voltage, with no measurable motion along $y$,
Figure~\ref{fig:donut-sweep-disp-eod34}; across the full $\pm10$~V, the $\pm200$~V of the
amplifier, it travels about $\pm48$~\textmu m, a little over half the ring radius. Throughout both
sweeps the cross-correlation with the control stays at or above 0.987, with a mean of 0.994,
Figure~\ref{fig:donut-sweep-ncc}. It falls by about one percent from the center of the range to
either end, alike for both EODs and symmetric in voltage. A decline of that form would follow
if the beam passed the lenses further from their axes at large deflections and picked up
aberration, or if the plate sat slightly off the plane conjugate to the EODs, so that a
deflection also moved the beam a little across it; these data do not isolate the cause. The extinction ratio shows no change at all within what the camera can
resolve, Figure~\ref{fig:donut-sweep-er}: it sits on the one-unit floor, near 0.04, across both
sweeps, as it does in the control at $0.035\pm0.016$.

EOD35 does not steer along the camera axes. Its sweep moves the donut by $4.33$~\textmu m/V along
$y$ but also by $-1.08$~\textmu m/V along $x$, Figure~\ref{fig:donut-sweep-disp-eod35}: a
deflection of $4.47$~\textmu m/V directed $14.0^\circ$ away from the $y$ axis. EOD34, with no
cross-motion, defines the camera axes, so the deflection axis of EOD35 is rotated by
$14^\circ$ about the beam relative to that of EOD34, and the two scan axes meet at about $76^\circ$
rather than at a right angle. A pattern computed on the assumption of orthogonal axes would be
sheared by that amount. Either the second EOD has to be turned in its mount to bring the
axes square, or the calibration has to carry both components of each EOD's motion rather
than a single sensitivity per axis. The rotation also bears on the polarization of
Section~\ref{sec:eod}, since the half-wave plate ahead of EOD35 must align the light to the crystal
axis as actually mounted. The two sensitivities measured here, 4.82 and 4.47~\textmu m/V, differ by
eight percent, and in the opposite sense from the six percent found at the perpendicular setting of
the polarization measurements. Each EOD's response depends on the polarization it receives in
this particular path, which is a reminder that the calibration belongs to the assembled optics rather
than to the EOD alone.

These displacements are left in micrometers at the camera, because the recorded geometry does not
account for their scale. With the camera at the focal plane of the 100~mm lens L7 and the beam
expanded 7.4 times ahead of it, an EOD delivering the 2.95~mrad/kV of Section~\ref{sec:eod}
should move the donut by about 0.8~\textmu m per volt of command; the measured 4.8~\textmu m/V is
six times that. The size of the ring disagrees in the same direction. An expanded beam of about
7~mm focused by a 100~mm lens would form a ring only a few micrometers in radius, under one pixel,
whereas the fitted ring radius is 86~\textmu m, some twenty times larger. Both discrepancies would
follow if the beam reaching L7 were much smaller than expected or if the camera sat away from the
focal plane, where a ring grows and a deflection moves it further, but neither has been checked.
Until the camera distance and the beam size at the plate are measured, these displacements cannot
be converted to deflection angles, and the ring radius describes the image on the camera rather
than the donut at a known focus.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}

The vortex plate forms a donut on this bench: a closed ring about 86~\textmu m in radius at the
camera, around a center that fits a vortex far better than a Gaussian. Its center is dark to within a
few percent of the ring, but this camera cannot say how much darker. With 8-bit values and rings of
12 to 50 units, the best bound is 0.03 and the one resolved value, $0.048\pm0.018$, sits on the
camera's floor. The quantity MINFLUX depends on most has therefore not yet been measured, only bounded.

The ring is uneven, and the evenness depends on the alignment. Its dominant distortion is a ring
brighter on one side, with the null displaced from the ring center by up to 41~\textmu m, which is
what a vortex plate off the beam center produces. The best alignment brings the variation around the
ring down to fifteen percent and the null to within 9~\textmu m of the center. Polarization, as
expected at low numerical aperture, did not change the shape in these frames.

The donut survives deflection. Across the full drive of either EOD its shape stays within about
one percent of the undeflected pattern and its null stays at the floor of what the camera can resolve, while it
moves linearly at 4.82 and 4.47~\textmu m/V. The one finding that needs action is geometric: the
deflection axis of EOD35 is rotated $14^\circ$ from that of EOD34, so the two scan axes are not
orthogonal, and either the mount or the calibration has to account for it. The absolute scale of
these numbers is less secure than their linearity: both the ring size and the displacement per volt
are many times larger than the recorded lens train predicts, so the geometry between the plate and
the camera needs to be measured before either is converted to the sample.

Five steps would close the gaps. The donut should be recorded with both EODs driven
together, across the two-dimensional pattern the instrument will use: every sweep here moved one
axis with the other held at zero, so the two-axis scanning named in the goals of this work
(Section~\ref{sec:goals}) has not yet been tested. The distance from L7 to the camera and the beam size at the plate
should be measured, so that the ring size and the displacements can be referred to a known focus
and converted to angle. The null should be measured with a detector that can resolve it: a
higher bit depth and longer or averaged exposures on the camera, or a photodiode behind a pinhole at
the center of the donut. The plate should be centered finely on the beam, using the one-sided
component of the ring as the error signal, and its axial position checked against the plane
conjugate to the EODs, so that deflection cannot move the beam across it. And the rotation of EOD35
should be removed in its mount or carried into a two-axis calibration. Two questions remain beyond
the bench altogether: whether the null survives the high numerical aperture of the objective, where
the handedness of the circular polarization decides it, and how deep it is at the sample.

\section{Galvanometer scanners}
\label{sec:galvo}
\subsection{Physical concept}

A galvanometer scanner steers a beam with a mirror that turns. The mirror is bonded to the shaft
of a small limited-rotation motor: a permanent-magnet rotor sits inside a fixed coil, and current
through the coil exerts a torque on the rotor proportional to that current. Nothing in that
arrangement fixes where the shaft points, so a position detector on the shaft measures its angle,
and a servo amplifier compares that angle with the commanded one and drives whatever coil current
closes the gap \cite{marshallHandbookOpticalLaser2012}. The command is a voltage, and in steady
state the mirror angle is proportional to it. The scanners used here are Cambridge Technology
6230K galvanometers, each driven by its own Model 671 analog servo, carrying 12~mm silicon mirrors.

The servo is what makes the device precise, and it also sets the limits on its precision. A
mirror that is held by feedback returns to the angle its detector reports, so its repeatability and
drift are those of the detector rather than of the mechanism: the manufacturer specifies a
short-term repeatability of 8~\textmu rad, a linearity of 99.9\% over $20^\circ$, and drifts of at
most 15~\textmu rad in zero and 50~ppm in scale per degree Celsius for this series. Its speed is limited by the opposite end of the
loop. The rotor and mirror have inertia, the coil current is finite, and the servo has to bring the
mirror to rest at the new angle without overshooting it, so a commanded step is followed by a delay
before the mirror moves, a rise as it swings, and a settling interval as it comes to rest. For
mirrors of this size these times are hundreds of microseconds, more than ten times as long as the
EODs of Section~\ref{sec:eod} take to settle completely and several hundred
times their initial response. This is the trade the instrument is built
around: the galvanometer is slow, but it can turn the beam through tens of degrees rather than
milliradians.

\paragraph{From mirror angle to beam angle.}
Turning a mirror through an angle $\theta$ turns the reflected beam through $2\theta$ when the
mirror turns about an axis perpendicular to the plane of incidence, the plane containing the
incoming and reflected beams. That doubling is why galvanometer angles are quoted in optical
degrees, twice the mechanical ones. It holds only for that orientation of the axis. A mirror at
an angle of incidence $i$, turned instead about an axis that lies in its own surface and in the
plane of incidence, tips the reflected beam out of that plane by $2\theta\cos i$, which for the
$45^\circ$ fold of a scanning mirror is $\sqrt{2}\,\theta$ rather than $2\theta$. The same rotation
also swings the beam slightly within the plane, by $\theta^2$, so a scan of this kind traces a
shallow parabola rather than a straight line. Which case
applies depends only on how the scanner is mounted relative to the beam, and it matters below.

The Model 671 converts command voltage to mirror angle at a fixed scale factor, which on these units
was set at the factory for a full scan of $40^\circ$ optical over the $\pm10$~V command range.
That is $2^\circ$ of optical deflection per volt, or $1^\circ$ of mechanical rotation per volt,
half the $2^\circ$ of mechanical rotation per volt of the driver's generic setting of $0.5$~V per
mechanical degree. The scale factor is what turns
the voltages written by the control software into beam angles, and so it is the first thing
measured below.

\paragraph{Two mirrors, one pivot.}
Two scanners are needed to steer in two dimensions, one per axis, and two mirrors cannot occupy
the same place. If the beam simply passed from one mirror to the next, the first mirror's
deflection would move the beam across the face of the second, and after both the beam would
pivot about two different points. A microscope needs the opposite: the scanned beam must pivot
about a single point that is imaged onto the back focal plane of the objective, so that tilting
the beam there translates the focus across the sample without moving the beam on the pupil and
clipping it \cite{pawleyHandbookBiologicalConfocal2006}. The standard remedy, and the one used
here, is a relay between the mirrors. Two lenses of focal length $f$, the first placed $f$ from
the first mirror and the second $f$ from the second mirror with $2f$ between them, form a 4f
system that images the first mirror onto the second at unit magnification. A beam leaving the first
mirror at angle $\theta$ arrives at the second mirror at its center at angle $-\theta$, whatever the
value of $\theta$, so both deflections pivot about the second mirror, and a further relay can carry
that single pivot to the objective.

\paragraph{From beam angle to position.}
An angle becomes a position wherever the beam meets a lens or a screen. A lens of focal length
$f$ maps a beam entering it at angle $\theta$ to a point a distance $f\tan\theta \approx f\theta$
from its axis in its back focal plane, independent of where the beam crosses the lens; a bare
detector a distance $z$ from the pivot sees the beam move by $z\theta$. The bench below uses the
first arrangement to calibrate angle against voltage and the second to follow the beam in time.

\paragraph{The role in the instrument.}
In the tracking scheme of Section~\ref{sec:minflux}, the galvanometer pair re-centers the whole
excitation pattern on the molecule after each position estimate, while the EODs probe within
it. Each localization is the sum of a position within the pattern and the position of the pattern
itself, and the galvanometer supplies the second term. Its calibration therefore enters every
trajectory as directly as the EODs' calibration does: a scale error stretches the track, a
drift walks it, and a step that has not settled moves the pattern while photons are being counted.

\subsection{Characterization}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The galvanometer pair is characterized to answer three questions, each tied to one way it enters
the tracking measurement. The first is how far the beam turns per volt on each axis. The control
software can only re-center the pattern by the amount it intends if it knows that scale, and the
two axes need not share it. The second is whether the mirror returns to the same angle when it
is commanded back to the same voltage, and whether that angle wanders with time. The
re-centering loop assumes that a given command puts the pattern at a given place; a mirror that
lands differently from one visit to the next, or drifts, adds its error to every localization in
the way the EOD drift of Section~\ref{sec:eod} would.

The third question is how long a move takes, and it bears on the design of the feedback loop
rather than on accuracy. The pattern cycle of the proposal is 100~\textmu s, while a galvanometer
step takes several hundred microseconds to complete. The re-centering can therefore run only once
every few pattern cycles, and the molecule keeps moving while it does. At the diffusion
coefficient of EGFR, about 0.3~\textmu m$^2$/s \cite{becerraQuantifyingProteinInteractions2024},
a molecule moves a distance $\sqrt{4Dt} \approx 19$~nm in the plane in 300~\textmu s, more than
half of a 30~nm pattern. The delay and settling of the mirror are thus the latency of the
tracking loop, and they decide how far the molecule can stray from the center of the pattern
before the pattern catches up.

\subsubsection{Setup}

The bench reproduces the scanning stage of the instrument on its own,
Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-setup}. The 642~nm source, the same laser as in Section~\ref{sec:eod}, is
folded by mirrors M1 and M2 onto the first scanner, G1, which steers the beam in $x$. Lenses L1
and L2, both of focal length 100~mm, form a 4f relay that images G1 onto the second scanner, G2,
which steers in $y$. After G2 the beam passes to one of two detectors. For the angle calibration
it passes through a third 100~mm lens, L3, onto the camera at its focal plane, with the
5.2~\textmu m pixels used throughout this work; the 100~mm focal length is then the conversion from
angle to position. For every measurement in time it falls instead on a Thorlabs PDQ80A quadrant
photodiode, 7.8~mm across, placed bare at a measured 200~mm from the scanner, whose difference outputs follow
the position of the spot without the frame rate of a camera.

The scanners are commanded in two ways. For the calibration, the return and the step response, a
TriggerScope~4 digital-to-analog converter writes the command voltage under the control of the
acquisition software, with G1 on its first channel and G2 on its second, and the quadrant
photodiode's outputs are read by an NI USB-6001 data acquisition card: $x$, $y$ and sum for the
slow measurements, and $x$ and $y$ alone for the step response, which needs the card's full
rate on two channels. For the
timing measurements, a Siglent SDG810 function generator drives the servo input directly and an
oscilloscope
records the generator's output on its first channel and the quadrant photodiode on its second.
Five measurements were made.

\paragraph{Angle calibration.}
Each axis in turn was swept from $-1$ to $+1$~V in steps of 0.01~V, one camera
frame per step, with the other axis held at 0~V. A narrower sweep of $\pm0.1$~V was recorded on
each axis.

\paragraph{Power cycling.}
The spot was recorded on the camera with the servo drivers powered off and then powered on with
both axes commanded to 0~V. The comparison was then repeated thirteen times on the quadrant photodiode, alternating off and on, with 3~s allowed for settling before each
reading.

\paragraph{Return and drift.}
Each axis was pulsed repeatedly from 0~V to 0.05~V and back, for twenty cycles,
with the other axis held at 0~V. The detector was read at both levels after 50~ms of settling, so
each cycle records one return to the same commanded position.

\paragraph{Step response.}
Each axis was driven with a 0.05~V square wave at 10, 50, 100, 200 and 300~Hz for 150
cycles, read on the quadrant photodiode at 10\,000 samples per second per channel. The command was
streamed point by point by the software, which reproduces the commanded frequency exactly on
average up to 300~Hz, although the timing of individual steps jitters; above 300~Hz the converter
drops commands, so the set stops there.

\paragraph{Delay.}
The delay was measured in two sets. In the large-step set, the function generator drove each axis
with a square wave of about 0.52~V peak to peak at 1, 100, 500 and 1000~Hz, with the oscilloscope
recording the generator on its first channel and the photodiode on its second. In the small-step
set, the measurement was repeated at 1~kHz with steps of 5, 10, 50 and 100~mV peak to peak, on both
axes, with the same two channels.

\subsubsection{Methods}

\paragraph{Angle from the camera.}
Each calibration frame is reduced to the centroid of the spot, taken over the pixels above a fifth
of its peak after subtracting the median level of the frame. The direction of the scan is found by
a straight-line fit to the centroids in two dimensions, and each centroid is resolved into a
displacement along that direction and one perpendicular to it. The displacement along the scan,
divided by the 100~mm focal length of L3, gives the optical angle, which is fitted against the
command voltage by a straight line; its slope is the scale factor of that axis in optical degrees
per volt. The residuals of that fit show whether the response is linear, and the perpendicular
displacement shows whether the scan is straight. Frames are excluded when the spot has left the
sensor or lies within a few spot widths of its edge, and three consecutive frames of the $x$ sweep
are excluded because they are about 30\% dimmer than their neighbors and smeared.

\paragraph{Position from the quadrant photodiode.}
The photodiode reports the difference between the light on opposite halves of its face. Divided by
the sum of all four quadrants, after subtracting the dark level, that difference becomes a
dimensionless position that is independent of laser power. It is not proportional to the position
of the spot over any great range. For a Gaussian spot of radius $w$ centered a distance $p$ from the
gap between the halves, the normalized signal is $\mathrm{erf}(\sqrt{2}\,p/w)$, which is linear only
while the spot straddles the gap and saturates at $\pm1$ once it lies wholly on one side. The spot
radius, 0.84~mm at the photodiode, is found by fitting that relation to the two commanded levels of
the return-and-drift runs, whose separation is known from the calibrated scale factor and the measured
200~mm standoff; the two axes give 0.834 and 0.849~mm. Positions are then converted to micrometers
with the local slope of the relation at each run's operating point, and the step responses are
linearized by the inverse error function before they are analyzed. A run whose sum signal falls
toward the dark level is rejected, since its position is then a ratio of noise to noise.

\paragraph{Repeatability and drift.}
For the return and power-cycling measurements, the readings at 0~V are averaged within each cycle and
a straight line is fitted through the per-cycle values against time. The scatter about that line is
the repeatability, and its slope is the drift. The two are kept apart because a slow walk of the
whole pattern and a cycle-to-cycle scatter are different failures with different remedies. Each is
judged against the measurement noise, taken from the scatter of the readings within a single dwell
while the mirror is held still.

\paragraph{Step response.}
A single cycle at 10\,000 samples per second contains only a handful of points across a transient of
a few hundred microseconds. Many cycles are therefore folded into one. Each edge is normalized to its
own two levels and aligned on its own crossing rather than on the nominal period, since the
software-paced command jitters by a fifth of a period or more. The edges are then realigned on the
folded template and averaged in 10~\textmu s bins. The sampling rate, nominally 10\,000 samples
per second, is set slightly off so that it does not divide the drive frequency; successive cycles
then sample different phases and fill in the transient. The
folded step gives the 10 to 90\% rise; the overshoot, as the largest excursion past the final level
in percent of the step; and the settling time, from the 10\% point to the last exit from a band of
$\pm2\%$ about the final level. The cycle-to-cycle scatter of a single sample is the noise floor
against which the overshoot is judged.

\paragraph{Delay.}
The timing is measured from the oscilloscope records. All the edges of a capture, from 9 to 25,
are aligned on the drive crossing the midpoint of its swing and averaged on the raw samples, without
smoothing. The averaged response gives the delay to 10\% and to 50\% of its swing and its 10 to
90\% rise, and the scatter between edges, by a jackknife over them, gives the uncertainty. Rising
and falling edges are kept apart. Whether the photodiode signal is inverted relative to the
command depends on the orientation of the detector, so its polarity is determined by sliding each polarity across the plausible range of delays and
keeping the better match; that range must stay below half a period, beyond which an inverted square
wave and a delayed one cannot be told apart.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{Angle calibration.}
Over the range in which the spot stays on the sensor, the beam angle follows the command voltage on
a straight line on both axes, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-cal-angle}. G1 deflects the beam by
$2.0287 \pm 0.0005^\circ$ per volt in $x$, and G2 by $1.4446 \pm 0.0001^\circ$ per volt in $y$
($R^2$ of 0.99999 and better, from 183 and 187 frames). Narrower
sweeps of $\pm0.1$~V on each axis give 2.031 and $1.453^\circ$ per volt. Below about $-0.87$~V the
spot runs off the edge of the camera and the frames carry no position; those points are not a
property of the scanners. At the camera these scale factors are 3.54~mm and 2.52~mm of displacement
per volt; at the quadrant photodiode at 200~mm, 7.08~mm and 5.04~mm per volt.

The $x$ value agrees within 1.5\% with the factory setting of $2^\circ$ per volt. The $y$ value is
28\% below it. Its ratio to the $x$ value, 0.712, is within 1\% of $\cos 45^\circ = 0.707$, the factor
by which a $45^\circ$ fold mirror turned about an axis in its plane of incidence deflects the beam
less than one turned about the perpendicular axis. On this bench both scanners fold the beam in the
plane of the table, G1 steering within that plane and G2 out of it, which is the geometry that
produces this factor. The two scanners therefore rotate equally per volt, as configured, and the
difference between the axes arises from how G2 meets the beam rather than from G2 itself. The same
geometry predicts a second, smaller effect, and it is there. As G2 tips the beam out of the plane it
also swings it slightly within it, so the $y$ scan bows sideways in proportion to the square of the
command, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-cal-bow}. The measured bow is $28.8 \pm 0.1$~\textmu m/V$^2$ at the
camera, against 31.8~\textmu m/V$^2$ predicted with no free parameter; the $x$ scan bows by only
2.4~\textmu m/V$^2$. The consequence for the instrument is that each axis needs its own measured
scale factor, and the generic $4^\circ$ of optical deflection per volt of the servo documentation
would overstate the
deflection by a factor of two in $x$ and nearly three in $y$.

The two axes also differ in linearity, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-cal-residuals}. The $y$ residuals
are 0.5~\textmu m rms with no curvature. The $x$ residuals are 6.2~\textmu m rms and follow a
shallow parabola, a quadratic term of $-0.0139^\circ$/V$^2$ that takes the local slope from
$2.05^\circ$ per volt at $-0.85$~V to $2.00^\circ$ per volt at $+1$~V. Only the $x$ deflection
passes through the L1--L2 relay, where it carries the beam up to 3.5~mm off the lens axis, and the
aberration of the relay is the likely cause, although a nonlinearity of G1 itself is not excluded.
Finally, the two scan directions meet at $82.4^\circ$ on the sensor rather than at a right angle. A
roll of the camera and a skew between the scanners cannot be told apart with these data, but either
way the control software should calibrate the two axes together, in two dimensions, as for the
rotated EOD axis found in Section~\ref{sec:donut}.

\paragraph{Power cycling.}
With the drivers powered off, the unheld mirrors come to rest 2.33~mm from their commanded zero on
the camera, an offset of $1.33^\circ$ in beam angle, equivalent to $-0.64$~V of $x$ command and
$-0.37$~V of $y$. Powered on again, they return to zero, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-onoff-landing}. Over
twelve settled power cycles on the quadrant photodiode in 4.5 minutes, the landing position scatters
about its trend by 0.29~\textmu m in $x$ and 0.47~\textmu m in $y$, or 1.5 and 2.4~\textmu rad of
beam angle, against a noise of 0.15 and 0.16~\textmu m per reading. The first reading after power-up
in that series had not settled and is excluded, and the readings with power off lie beyond the
linear range of the photodiode, so the camera gives the offset. Losing power is therefore not a loss
of calibration: the servo brings each mirror back to the angle its own detector reads as zero, to
within a few microradians. The landing position in $y$ also walked steadily, by 1.0~\textmu m per
minute, 4.7~\textmu m over the series, while $x$ moved by only 0.12~\textmu m per minute.

\paragraph{Return and drift.}
Commanded repeatedly away and back with power held on, both mirrors return to nearly the same place,
Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-drift-return}. Over twenty returns in 3.6 minutes, the return position
scatters about its trend by 0.22~\textmu m in $x$ and 0.63~\textmu m in $y$, against a standard
error of 0.12 and 0.14~\textmu m for each dwell; that is 1.1 and 3.2~\textmu rad of beam angle,
within the 8~\textmu rad repeatability specified for these scanners. Superposed on that is a slow
walk. In $x$ the return position drifts by $-0.44$~\textmu m per minute, $-1.5$~\textmu m over the
run and several times its own scatter; in $y$ it drifts by only 0.06~\textmu m per minute, within
the scatter. The walk in $x$, about 7~\textmu rad over the run, is of the size the specified zero
drift of 15~\textmu rad per degree Celsius would give for half a degree of warming, and the faster
walk in $y$ after the power cycles is consistent with the same cause. A walk of that kind moves the
whole pattern without degrading it, and is the kind of error that an independent drift reference
\cite{lopezOpensourceSubNanometerStabilization2025} removes.

\paragraph{Step response.}
Both axes follow a commanded step cleanly at every frequency measured,
Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-step-100hz}. At 100~Hz the step rises from 10 to 90\% in 204~\textmu s in
$x$ and 214~\textmu s in $y$, overshoots by 1.64 and 0.43\%, and settles to within $\pm2\%$ of its
final level 254 and 284~\textmu s after its 10\% point. From 10 to 300~Hz the overshoot is 1.6 to
1.8\% in $x$ and 0.33 to 0.49\% in $y$, against a noise floor of about 0.2\%, and it does not change
with frequency, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-step-overshoot}. Nor does the settling time, 240 to
258~\textmu s in $x$ and 274 to 288~\textmu s in $y$, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-step-settling}, or the
rise, 198 to 205 and 211 to 218~\textmu s. That is what a property of the mirror and its servo
should do. Rising and falling steps agree once the photodiode is linearized: without it, the
compression of the detector near the edge of its range makes the $x$ overshoot read 1.33\% on rising
and 1.78\% on falling edges, and with it 1.63 and 1.68\%. The two axes are not identical, $x$
overshooting about four times more but settling about 30~\textmu s sooner than $y$, but neither is
the weaker in every respect.

\paragraph{Delay.}
The small-step set keeps the spot within the linear range of the photodiode and gives the
delay directly, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-delay-amplitude}. Measured from the drive's midpoint, the
response reaches 10\% of its swing after 152 to 168~\textmu s and 50\% after 246 to
261~\textmu s, on both axes, and its 10 to 90\% rise takes 201 to 213~\textmu s,
Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-rise-amplitude}, in agreement with the folded steps of the step-response measurement. None of
these changes with the size of the step from 10 to 100~mV: the edge has the same shape at every
amplitude, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-delay-edges}, as expected of a linear servo far from its current
limit. The 5~mV step in $x$ is at the noise floor and is not resolved.

The large-step set behaves differently, and the reason is the detector. A 0.52~V step
moves the spot 3.7~mm in $x$ and 2.6~mm in $y$ at the photodiode, several times its 0.84~mm radius,
so the signal saturates at both ends and changes only while the spot crosses the gap between the
quadrants. Its 50\% point marks that crossing, and its 10 to 90\% time, 75 to 129~\textmu s, is the
time taken to cross the gap rather than the rise of the mirror. If the swing is not centered on the
gap, rising and falling steps cross it at different fractions of their travel and so at different
times. That is the origin of the apparent asymmetry in $x$, Figure~\ref{fig:galvo-delay-frequency},
where rising steps cross at 312~\textmu s and falling steps at 240~\textmu s: the same split appears,
smaller, at 50 and 100~mV in the small-step set and vanishes at 10~mV, so it belongs to the detector and
not to the axis. Averaged over the two directions, the crossing comes 276 to 278~\textmu s after the
drive in $x$ and 270 to 275~\textmu s in $y$, the same at 100~Hz, 500~Hz and 1~kHz, about
20~\textmu s later than the 50\% point of the small steps. The 1~Hz records were sampled at 1~ms
intervals, longer than the delay itself, and cannot resolve it.

Both sets show a fixed delay. A fixed delay is a phase lag that grows in proportion to frequency,
$10^\circ$ at 100~Hz but $99^\circ$ at 1~kHz, where the beam is a quarter of a cycle behind the
command. It has to be compensated as a time offset, the same at every rate, not as a phase fitted at
each frequency.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}

The galvanometer pair is well calibrated and repeatable, and the measurements give the numbers its
control software needs; its speed is the limit, as the end of this conclusion sets out. Each axis turns the beam in proportion to its command, but the
two axes do not share a scale factor: $2.029^\circ$ per volt in $x$ against $1.445^\circ$ in $y$.
The $y$ value is smaller by $\cos 45^\circ$ because G2 tips the beam out of the plane in which it
folds it, not because of a fault in the scanner, and the sideways bow of the $y$ scan matches the
same geometry with no adjustable parameter. Neither value matches the generic factor of the servo
documentation, so the measured values, not the nominal one, must be written into the control
software, and the $y$ value must be measured again whenever the scanners are remounted. The $x$
response bends by about 2.5\% in slope across $\pm1$~V, probably in the relay, and the two scan
directions are $7.6^\circ$ from perpendicular on the sensor, so the calibration should be made in
two dimensions rather than one axis at a time.

Positioning is repeatable to a few microradians of beam angle, both after a commanded move and after
power has been removed and restored, within the scanners' specification. The slow walks seen, about
2~\textmu rad per minute in $x$ with power held and about 5~\textmu rad per minute in $y$ after power
cycling, are consistent with the specified thermal drift of the scanners. They move the pattern as a
whole and are best removed by an independent drift correction rather than by the galvanometer
calibration, and the scanners should be left powered and warm before a measurement.

A move takes a few hundred microseconds. Measured from the command, the mirror reaches a tenth of a
small step after about 160~\textmu s and half of it after about 250~\textmu s, covers 10 to 90\% of
it in about 200~\textmu s, and is within $\pm2\%$ of its final position 240 to 290~\textmu s after
it began to move, with an overshoot of 1.7\% in $x$ and 0.4\% in $y$. The whole move is therefore
complete 0.4 to 0.45~ms after the command, the same for both directions and for every step size
measured. That fixes the latency of the re-centering loop at four or five pattern cycles of
100~\textmu s, during which a molecule diffusing at the rate of EGFR moves about 23~nm in the plane,
most of a 30~nm pattern. The loop must therefore either wait for each move to settle before
trusting the pattern position again, or carry the fixed delay of each axis in its model of where
the pattern is. Small corrections may be better made by offsetting the pattern with the
EODs, if their range at the sample allows, leaving the galvanometers the larger moves. The direction-dependent delay that
the large steps appeared to show belongs to the detector and needs no compensation.

Two things remain open. The curvature of the $x$ response should be traced, by recentering the beam
in the relay and repeating the sweep. And the scale factors must be carried from beam angle at the
scanner to displacement at the sample, which depends on the relay to the objective and is outside
this bench.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This work set out to establish whether the beam-steering hardware of a MINFLUX tracking instrument
meets the requirements of Section~\ref{sec:goals}, and to measure the parameters its control system
needs. Taken goal by goal, the results are as follows.

The first goal, characterizing the EODs, is met. Both deflect linearly across the full
$\pm200$~V, by 2.95 and 3.12~mrad/kV once the input polarization is set perpendicular to the
deflection axis, which makes the half-wave plate part of the calibration rather than an alignment
detail. Their six percent difference means each needs its own calibration. Held at a fixed drive,
EOD35 adds no measurable wander to that of the laser, while EOD34 drifts by up to 0.03~mrad near
zero and negative drive, a behavior still to be explained. They begin to move half a microsecond
after the drive and are within the noise of their target by the end of the 25~\textmu s dwell, but
not within a small fraction of it: the slow second stage leaves EOD34 up to a fifth of a step
short after 5~\textmu s and still a few percent short after 10~\textmu s. The first microseconds of
each dwell therefore need either to be discarded or to be corrected by shaping the drive.

The second goal, the control interface, is met for calibration and slow control. The data
acquisition card drives both amplifiers across their full range once a voltage follower stands
between them, and the impedance mismatch that first limited the drive is understood. The card is
too slow to switch the pattern, so that task falls to the instrument's FPGA path, whose timing the
step-response measurements already describe from the amplifier onward.

The third goal, the donut under deflection, is met in part. The charge-one vortex plate forms a
donut whose shape changes by about one percent under the full deflection of either axis, so
deflection does not visibly degrade it. Two parts of the goal remain open. The depth of the null is
bounded only by the camera, at 3 to 5\% of the ring peak, and each axis was deflected alone, never in the
two-axis pattern itself. The same measurement found the two deflection axes $14^\circ$ from
perpendicular, which the calibration must carry.

The galvanometers, added to the original plan, re-center the pattern with a well-determined scale,
$2.03^\circ$ and $1.44^\circ$ per volt, and return to within a few microradians. Their move,
complete 0.4 to 0.45~ms after the command, is longer than the 0.2~ms in which a diffusing receptor
wanders half the pattern. Small corrections may therefore be better made by offsetting the pattern
with the EODs, if their range at the sample allows, and the galvanometers kept for the larger
moves, the division of labor used in MINFLUX on a standard microscope stand
\cite{schmidtMINFLUXNanometerscale3D2021}.

Measured against the requirements of Section~\ref{sec:goals}, the picture is mixed. The EOD
calibration is known to about two percent, limited by the lever arm, close to but not yet at the
one percent asked for. EOD35 holds its position as well as the laser allows, but EOD34 drifts.
An EOD step is complete within the dwell but not within a small fraction of it. The depth of
the null is bounded only at 3 to 5\% of the ring peak. And the galvanometer move is longer than the
time a receptor takes to wander half the pattern. Whether the remaining errors amount to a fraction
of a nanometer at the sample depends on the scale there, which this bench does not reach.

The operating parameters established here are collected in Tables~\ref{tab:eod-parameters}
and~\ref{tab:galvo-parameters}. Every scale in them is referred to the bench, at the EOD or the
scanner. Carrying them to the sample through the relay and objective is the step that joins this
characterization to the tracking measurement itself.

\section*{AI use}
\label{sec:ai-use}

This work used Claude Code (Anthropic), an AI coding assistant built on the Claude language
models, in two ways.

For the analysis software, I worked out what each analysis had to do and how it should be done,
and Claude Code helped write the Julia code to that plan. It also reviewed the code, found and
fixed bugs, committed and pushed the changes to the project repositories, and produced the graphs
in a consistent style.

For this document, Claude Code helped draft and revise the text from my notes, data and
explanations. It corrected spelling, formatting and LaTeX syntax, drew the schematic in
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-concept}, and pointed out explanations that were unclear, disagreed with the
data or were physically wrong, which I then corrected or confirmed.

The experimental work is my own. I designed and built the optical setups, aligned them, chose what
to measure and how, and took all the data reported here.

I checked everything Claude Code produced, including code, graphs and text, against my data, my lab
notes and the cited sources before keeping it. I take full responsibility for the content of this
thesis and for any errors in it.
