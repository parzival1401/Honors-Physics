\section{EOD}
\subsection{Physical concept}

An electro-optic deflector steers a laser beam without moving anything. It is a transparent
crystal with electrodes on its faces; applying a voltage changes the crystal's refractive index,
and that change alone is enough to turn the beam. This subsection sets out why that works, and
why it is worth the trouble it costs.

Light travels more slowly where the refractive index is higher. A beam of finite width can be
pictured as carrying a wavefront, the surface joining the points that are in step with one
another, and the beam travels in whatever direction that surface faces. Delay one side of the
beam more than the other and the surface tilts; the beam then leaves in a new direction.
Steering a beam therefore needs nothing more than a refractive index that differs across the
beam's width, and the tilt grows with both the size of that difference and the length of crystal
over which it acts.

The applied field is what produces the difference. In lithium tantalate the index shift is
proportional to the field, so doubling the voltage doubles the deflection and reversing the
voltage reverses it. A uniform field across a uniform slab, however, shifts the index everywhere
by the same amount: the whole wavefront is delayed, nothing tilts, and the device is a phase
modulator rather than a deflector. The index change has to vary across the aperture, and a
deflector is built so that it does, by dividing the crystal into sections whose orientation is
opposed, so that a single applied voltage raises the index on one side of the beam while lowering
it on the other.

What makes this convenient is that nothing has to be accelerated. A galvanometer steers by
turning a mirror, and how quickly it can move and settle is set by the inertia of that mirror and
by the servo holding it in place. An electro-optic deflector has no moving part at all, so its
speed is set instead by how fast charge can be pushed onto the electrodes, which is a question of
the crystal's capacitance and the amplifier driving it. The limit moves from mechanics to
electronics, and electronics is much the faster of the two. Two consequences follow. There is no
settling oscillation to wait out, and no wear or vibration to manage. And because there is no
mass to accelerate, the time to move between two positions scarcely depends on how far apart they
are: positions can be addressed in any order at the same cost, where a mirror has to travel.

That last property is why the technique suits this instrument. MINFLUX does not raster a beam
across a field; it parks the excitation at a small number of known positions and compares the
photon counts collected at each. Time spent moving and settling is time not spent collecting
photons, and the positions are revisited in rapid alternation rather than swept in sequence, so a
deflector that jumps between them at electronic speed is worth a great deal more than one that
covers a wide angle slowly.

The price is paid in three places. The angles available are small, milliradians rather than the
tens of degrees a mirror gives, so the deflection has to be magnified and relayed by the optics
that follow. The drive voltages are high, which is why each crystal is fed from a high-voltage
amplifier rather than directly from a control card. And the response is referred to the crystal's
own axes, so how much deflection a given voltage produces depends on how the input polarization
is oriented with respect to those axes; polarization becomes part of the calibration rather than
a detail of the alignment, and is why a half-wave plate appears between the two deflectors in the
arrangements described later.

It is worth naming the alternative, because it behaves quite differently. A second family of
deflectors, built on potassium tantalate niobate, KTa$_{1-x}$Nb$_x$O$_3$ (KTN), reaches far
larger angles, 250~mrad from a few millimetres of crystal
\cite{nakamuraSpacechargecontrolledElectroopticEffect2008}, and does so by a different route: the
index gradient comes from electrons injected into the crystal, whose accumulated charge shapes
the internal field \cite{nakamuraSpacechargecontrolledElectroopticEffect2008,bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012}.
Those devices are fast and wide-ranging
\cite{bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012,miyazu400KHzBeam2010,yagiElectroopticKTNDevices2014},
but what they do depends on charge that has been put there and can move, so their behaviour
drifts with operating conditions \cite{bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012}. The deflectors used
here are not of that type. Their response comes from the crystal itself rather than from injected
charge, which is why the deflection is expected to follow the applied voltage proportionally, and
why that proportionality is something the measurements reported below can meaningfully test.
Against mechanical scanners, both solid-state families trade angular range for addressing rate,
exceeding conventional mirror scanners by up to an order of magnitude on the latter
\cite{romerElectroopticAcoustoopticLaser2014,heberleElectroopticAcoustoopticLaser2016}.

Concretely, the instrument carries two Leysop \texttt{ED2-2T-LT-AR561-642} deflectors, one per
transverse axis, with a 2~mm clear aperture in a 60~mm housing and an antireflection coating
covering both the 561 and 642~nm excitation lines. Each is driven single-ended through two MHV
electrode inputs by a Thorlabs HVA200 high-voltage amplifier ($\times20$, $\pm200$~V), which is
fed in turn from the FPGA through a digital-to-analog converter and an op-amp preamplifier stage.
The vendor supplies no datasheet for these semi-custom units; the only performance figure given
in writing is a quoted minimum deflection sensitivity of ${>}3$~mrad/kV, and the deflection pivot
is stated to lie at the centre of the crystal. Everything else used by the control system is
established by the measurements reported below.

The two deflectors are referred to throughout by serial number. EOD34 is the first in the beam
path, steers the $x$ axis, and is driven by amplifier HVA1; EOD35 follows it, steers $y$, and is
driven by HVA2. Every result below is attributed to one or the other by these names.

\subsection{Characterization}

\subsubsection{Motivation}

The EOD pair is what moves the excitation pattern between the positions of a MINFLUX cycle, and the
control system commands that motion in volts while the experiment is defined in nanometres at the
sample. Everything in this subsection exists to establish that conversion and to bound how far it
can be trusted. The need is sharper here than for a catalogue component, because these are
semi-custom units for which the manufacturer supplies no datasheet: the single performance figure
available in writing is a quoted minimum sensitivity of ${>}3$~mrad/kV. A quoted minimum is not a
calibration. It cannot say whether EOD34 and EOD35 match, whether the response is linear across the
drive range, how the sensitivity depends on input polarization, or how quickly the deflection
settles after a commanded step, and each of those enters the position of the excitation pattern
directly.

Linearity is the first of these. The scan pattern is only faithful if the mapping from drive voltage
to deflection angle is proportional across the range actually used, and a departure from
proportionality displaces the pattern relative to where the control system believes it to be. The
consequences are not hypothetical: in high-resolution force-clamp trapping, the nonlinear response
of acousto-optic deflectors introduced experimental artifacts that were removed by substituting
electro-optic deflectors \cite{woodyElectroopticDeflectorsDeliver2018}. The same logic applies
within a single EOD across its own drive range.

Polarization is the second, and it is specific to the linear electro-optic mechanism described
above. Because the response is referred to the crystal axes, the sensitivity obtained depends on the
orientation of the input polarization, and the rotational alignment of each deflector to its crystal
axis is therefore a calibration parameter rather than a one-time alignment step. This is the reason
the input polarization appears among the quantities varied below.

Timing is the third. The photon economy of MINFLUX is what opens the microsecond regime in which
molecular jumps and conformational changes become observable
\cite{eilersMINFLUXMonitorsRapid2018}, and reaching that regime on a conventional microscope stand
has depended specifically on synchronized electro-optical and galvanometric beam steering together
with sub-nanometer sample stabilization \cite{schmidtMINFLUXNanometerscale3D2021}. The requirement
this places on the deflector is that the step settling of the whole drive chain be small compared
with the dwell at each position of the pattern, so that the excitation is stationary during every
sub-exposure. Whether that holds is a property of the amplifier and preamplifier as much as of the
crystal, which is why the characterization below treats the drive chain rather than the deflector
alone.

Beam quality is the fourth, and it is a question about what the EOD hands downstream. The deflectors
sit ahead of the element that shapes the excitation pattern, so a Gaussian beam that leaves them
distorted, clipped by the 2~mm aperture, or displaced as a function of deflection angle compromises
the pattern formed later in the path. Profile deformation that depends on the deflection midpoint
and amplitude has been documented for electro-optic scanning, albeit in a different crystal system
and with femtosecond pulses, where fluence was the dominant variable
\cite{bechtoldBeamProfileDeformation2012}; the fluence dependence is not expected to transfer to the
present continuous-wave regime, but the deflection-angle dependence is exactly the behaviour the
beam-quality measurements below are designed to detect.

Finally, the geometry through which an angle is inferred has to be measured rather than assumed.
Deflection angle is not observed directly but reconstructed from a displacement on a camera and the
distance from the crystal to that camera, so an error in the assumed lever arm propagates in full
into the quoted sensitivity. An earlier calibration in this project was withdrawn for exactly this
reason, and the measurements reported here refer the lever arm explicitly to the deflection
pivot at the centre of the crystal rather than to the face of its housing.

\subsubsection{Setup}

All four measurements record the beam directly on the camera sensor, and each reduces the
recorded frames by the procedures set out under Methods.

\paragraph{Beam quality across the deflector aperture.}
A single deflector is mounted on a micrometer stage after two folding mirrors and translated
across the beam, so that the beam samples the crystal from one edge of its 2~mm aperture to the
other. A frame is recorded at each stage position, and a control frame recorded on the same
bench serves as the reference. EOD34 and EOD35 are measured in turn. The arrangement is that of
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-setup-beam-degradation}.

as a function of the commanded deflection instead of being characterised at a single setting.
The sweep is quoted throughout as the command voltage, $\pm10$~V, which the high-voltage
amplifier raises by a factor of twenty to the $\pm200$~V appearing across the crystal electrodes.
An undeflected control frame is recorded for each deflector. The arrangement is that of

\paragraph{Deflection sensitivity and polarization.}
On the same bench, EOD34 and EOD35 are each swept across roughly $\pm200$~V of amplifier output
while the camera records the beam centre at every step. The sweep is repeated at three input
polarizations, set by a linear polarizer ahead of the crystal: parallel to the deflection axis,
perpendicular to it, and with the polarizer removed. The crystal-to-sensor distance, which
converts displacement into angle, is measured on the bench as 190~mm from the face of the
deflector housing; the analysis refers it to the deflection pivot at the centre of the crystal,
30~mm further back, for a lever arm of 220~mm.

\subsubsection{Methods}

Every measurement in this section reduces a camera frame to a few numbers describing the beam,
so the reduction is set out once here and then referred to. All lengths are converted from pixels
using a sensor pitch of 5.2~\textmu m.

\paragraph{Preparing a frame.}
A background level is formed as the mean of the dimmest five percent of pixels in the frame and
subtracted uniformly, and the result is clipped at zero so that noise below background cannot
contribute negative weight. The beam centre is then located by a thresholded centroid rather than
by the brightest pixel. The distinction is not cosmetic: because the centre selects which row and
column become the measured profiles, and the peak of those profiles sets the half-maximum
reference, a single hot pixel taken as the centre propagates into every width derived from that
frame.

\paragraph{Measuring a width.}
Profiles through the centre are averaged over a band of eleven lines either side rather than read
from a single line. For a separable Gaussian this is exact: averaging symmetric rows returns the
same transverse profile scaled by a constant, so the width is preserved while the noise falls as
the square root of the number of lines. The frames here peak at only tens of counts, and on a
single line one noise dip is enough to terminate the width search early. The averaged profile is
then smoothed with a symmetric box filter three pixels either side, chosen because a zero-phase
filter cannot move the position of a crossing.

The width is taken as the contiguous run of the profile that lies above half of its maximum
\emph{and contains the centre}, with both crossings linearly interpolated between the bracketing
samples. Searching instead for the first and last sample anywhere above half maximum, which is
the obvious implementation, is not equivalent: a second spot elsewhere in the line, or a single
bright pixel, stretches the reported width from one to the other.

Ellipticity is the ratio of the larger to the smaller of the two axis widths, so unity denotes a
circular beam.

\paragraph{Fitting a Gaussian.}
A circularly symmetric Gaussian on a constant pedestal,
\begin{equation}
    I(x,y) = C \exp\!\left(-\frac{2\left[(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2\right]}{\omega^2}\right) + b ,
\end{equation}
is fitted by Nelder--Mead least squares, to at most five thousand iterations, initialised from
the measured peak amplitude, the centroid, the background level, and a radius taken from the
profile width. The model carries no rotation angle and no separate widths along the two axes; it
is the ellipticity above, not the fit, that reports departure from circularity.

The fit runs on a square crop about the centre whose half-side is two and a half times the
starting radius, floored at 250~pixels. Scaling the crop to the beam matters: at the expanded end
of this bench the spot is several hundred pixels across, and a crop that holds less than the core
drives the coefficient of determination down to a value that reads as "not Gaussian" when it
means only that the window was too small to contain the Gaussian. The reported $R^2$ is computed
against that crop.

\paragraph{Two radii, and why they are reported together.}
With the model written as above, $\omega$ is the radius at which the intensity falls to $1/e^2$
of its peak, and for that convention the width at half maximum and the radius are related by
$\mathrm{FWHM} = \omega\sqrt{2\ln 2}$. A second radius is therefore obtained from the measured
width alone, as the mean of the two axis widths divided by $\sqrt{2\ln 2} \approx 1.1774$,
without reference to the fit.

For a true Gaussian the two must agree, which is the point of computing both. They separate when
the profile carries wings the model does not describe, so the gap between them is a measurement
of how far the beam departs from Gaussian rather than a disagreement between two estimates of the
same thing. The constant is convention-sensitive: the factor $2\sqrt{\ln 2}$ belongs to the
alternative definition $I \propto \exp(-r^2/\omega^2)$, and using it beside a $1/e^2$ fit
separates the two radii by a flat factor of $\sqrt{2}$ for purely notational reasons.

\paragraph{Calibrating deflection against drive.}
The high-voltage amplifier presents a monitor output scaled to one twentieth of its true output
and inverted; the drive applied to the crystal is recovered by negating that reading and
multiplying by twenty. Each sweep step records this monitor value together with the commanded
output and a frame.

Deflection is obtained from the displacement of the beam centre across the sweep. With the
crystal-to-sensor distance $L$ as the lever arm, the angle follows from the displacement as
$\theta \simeq \Delta x / L$ in the small-angle limit, which at the milliradian angles here is
indistinguishable from the exact inverse tangent. The sensitivity is not read from the endpoints
but from an ordinary least-squares regression of beam centre against recovered drive voltage
across the whole sweep, expressed in milliradians per kilovolt and compared against the
manufacturer's quoted minimum of 3~mrad/kV.

\paragraph{Comparing a frame against the control.}
Where a frame is compared with an undeflected reference, both are mean-subtracted and their
two-dimensional cross-correlation is computed by Fourier transform, normalised by the square root
of the product of their summed squares, so that a peak of unity denotes identical frames up to a
shift. The peak value measures how far the beam still resembles the reference, and the location
of that peak relative to zero lag gives the displacement between them.

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{Beam quality across the deflector aperture.}
Here EOD34 and EOD35 are each translated across the beam on the stage. The two are the same
model and identical in construction, so differences between their traces reflect the individual
crystals and their mounting rather than design. The clearest result of this measurement is a
working range rather than a degradation curve. The
correlation against the undeflected control, Figure~\ref{fig:eod-beam-xcorr-ncc}, rises to
essentially unity over a plateau running from roughly 10.5 to 12~mm of stage travel and falls
away steeply on both sides, reaching 0.2 to 0.4 at either extreme. Across that plateau the width
of the correlation peak, Figure~\ref{fig:eod-beam-xcorr-fwhm}, is flat and sits on the control
value, so where the beam is usable at all it is no less sharp than the reference. The
displacement, Figures~\ref{fig:eod-beam-xcorr-dx} and~\ref{fig:eod-beam-xcorr-dy}, behaves the
same way: flat within the plateau and running to several hundred pixels in $x$ outside it, while
$y$ stays near zero throughout. EOD35 reaches the plateau about 0.7~mm of travel earlier than
EOD34, but the two leave it together.

\paragraph{Beam quality along the path.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-path} follows the beam from the fibre output through EOD34, the first relay,
EOD35 and the second relay. Because the stations sit at different distances along the path, the
absolute widths of Figure~\ref{fig:eod-path-fwhm} change partly through ordinary propagation; the ellipticity and the
fit quality are the measures of shape, and they are read here against the control rather than
against an ideal. The control at P1 is very nearly circular, with an ellipticity of 1.01, and its
Gaussian fit reaches an $R^2$ of 0.965, which sets the ceiling for the later stations.

EOD34 is where the shape changes most. Between P1 and P2 the width along $x$, its deflection
axis, grows by about a quarter, from roughly 570 to 720~\textmu m, while the width along $y$ grows
by under a tenth, and the ellipticity rises to 1.17. Across the first relay and EOD35 together,
P2 to P4, the beam rounds again to an ellipticity of 1.08, but the fit quality falls to
0.90, so the profile moves further from Gaussian even as it becomes more nearly circular. The
second relay, P4 to P5, leaves the fit quality unchanged but exchanges the axes: the width along
$x$ returns to the control value while that along $y$ keeps growing, and the ellipticity reaches
1.19, now elongated along $y$. At every measurable station before the amplification stage the
ellipticity therefore stays below 1.2, and the loss of Gaussian character, from 0.965 to 0.90,
is accumulated entirely through EOD34, the first relay and EOD35.

Station P6 is reported but not interpreted. After the amplification stage the beam is more than
six times wider than at any earlier station, which is the expansion that stage exists to provide,
but its peak reaches only 9 counts, below the 15-count level at which the half-maximum search
rises clear of the noise, so the width, the ellipticity and the fit at P6 are not reliable
measures of shape. Each station is also a single frame, so differences of a few percent between
stations are not distinguishable from frame-to-frame scatter.

\paragraph{Beam quality against deflection.}
Driving the deflector changes the shape of the beam, but modestly. In
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-quality-voltage-fwhm} the widths along the two transverse axes of each
deflector move in opposite senses as the drive advances, one broadening while the other narrows,
which is a change of shape rather than of size. The ellipticity of
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-quality-voltage-ellipticity} puts a number on it: at worst about 14 percent
from circular, reached by EOD34 at the positive end of the sweep. The two deflectors do not
behave alike. EOD34 departs from circular progressively toward positive drive, while EOD35 is
furthest from circular near the middle of its range and returns to nearly circular at the
positive extreme.

Two independent measures agree that the profile is not exactly Gaussian. The fit quality of
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-quality-voltage-r2} lies between about 0.94 and 0.97 across the entire sweep
for both EOD34 and EOD35, never approaching unity. More tellingly, the two radii of
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-quality-voltage-omega} differ by roughly a tenth to a seventh of their value
throughout, in the same direction for both deflectors. Those two quantities are the same radius
computed two ways and must coincide for a true Gaussian, so their separation measures wings the
fitted model does not describe. The gap is far smaller than the factor of $\sqrt{2}$ that a
mismatch of radius conventions would produce, so it is a property of the beam rather than of the
arithmetic.

\paragraph{Deflection sensitivity and its polarization dependence.}
Each deflector steers along a single axis: the beam centre moves linearly in $x$ on the camera
across the full $\pm200$~V of amplifier output while $y$ remains flat, in panels (b) and (c) of
Figure~\ref{fig:eod-polarization-hva1} for EOD34 and Figure~\ref{fig:eod-polarization-hva2} for
EOD35. EOD35 shows its three polarization traces displaced from one another in $y$ by a few
pixels, but each is flat with drive, so this is a fixed offset between configurations rather than
a response to the applied field. Linearity holds across the whole range for both deflectors; no
departure from a straight line is visible at either extreme.

The sensitivity depends strongly on the input polarization, panel (d) of each figure. With the
polarization perpendicular to the deflection axis, EOD34 gives 2.95~mrad/kV and EOD35 3.12;
unpolarized, 2.08 and 2.77; and parallel to the deflection axis, 0.83 and 0.88. The ratio between
the best and worst polarization is about 3.5 for both, so this is a property of the device type
rather than of one crystal.

Measured against the manufacturer's quoted minimum of 3~mrad/kV, the outcome depends on the
polarization first and on the deflector second. Only at the perpendicular setting do the
deflectors approach the specification at all: EOD35 meets it, and EOD34 falls short by under two
percent, a margin comparable to the uncertainty in the lever arm discussed below. Unpolarized,
both fall below it, and at the parallel setting both fail by a factor of
about three and a half. A half-wave plate correctly set is therefore a precondition for the
deflectors reaching their quoted performance, not an optimization applied on top of it.

Two consequences follow for the control system. The orientation of the input polarization has to
be fixed and recorded as part of the calibration, since it alone moves the volts-to-angle
conversion across that range. And EOD34 and EOD35 cannot be assumed identical: unpolarized, EOD35
is a third more sensitive than EOD34, so a scan pattern built on a single sensitivity for both
axes would be distorted.

Two cautions attach to these numbers. They are inversely proportional to the lever arm, taken
here as 220~mm: the 190~mm measured on the bench runs from the housing face to the sensor, and
the manufacturer places the deflection pivot at the centre of the 60~mm housing, a further
30~mm back. Referring the angle to the housing face instead would overstate every sensitivity by
about sixteen percent and carry both deflectors above the specification at the perpendicular
setting, so where that distance is measured from is not a detail. And the first recorded point
of every sweep, at the negative extreme of the drive, sits off the line established by the
remainder; it has the character of a settling transient and is excluded from the regressions.

\subsection{characterization}

\subsubsection{intro why}

MINFLUX determines an emitter's position by probing it with an intensity minimum rather than by
collecting the full diffraction pattern of its fluorescence, and this is what allows it to reach
single-digit nanometer precision while detecting ten to a hundred times fewer photons than
centroid-based localization
\cite{balzarottiNanometerResolutionImaging2017,eilersMINFLUXMonitorsRapid2018}. The same property is
the method's vulnerability. Because the position estimate is referred to the zero of the excitation
pattern, the quality of that zero is not a cosmetic concern but enters the measurement directly,
which is why the generation and optimization of toroidal foci is treated as a discipline of its own
\cite{lopezOptimizationCharacterizationToroidal2023}.

Conditions that distort the excitation pattern produce not merely a noisier estimate but a biased
one: simulations of fixed absorption dipoles and optical aberrations in MINFLUX find systematic
localization errors reaching roughly a quarter of the diameter of the circle spanned by the doughnut
centre positions \cite{stallingaAbsorptionDipoleEffects2026}. An error of that character cannot be
averaged away by collecting more photons, which is what distinguishes a compromised null from a
merely noisy measurement and what makes the depth and symmetry of the null worth measuring in their
own right.

A further limitation is that the null must survive being moved. The pattern is translated across the
field by the deflectors characterized in the previous section, and a null that degrades with
deflection would make the localization bias a function of scan position. Residual drift bears on the
same quantity from the other direction, since techniques that hold the sample fixed within an
excitation pattern require active drift correction to approach their theoretical limits at all
\cite{lopezOpensourceSubNanometerStabilization2025}.

\subsubsection{methods}
\begin{itemize}
    \item background subtraction (bottom 5\% pixels)
    \item center detection: centroid of bright ring pixels (30\% peak threshold) — dark null coincides with ring centroid
    \item ring radius: peak location on cross-profile through center, averaged left/right ($r_x$) and top/bottom ($r_y$)

    \item donut profile fitting
    \begin{itemize}
        \item LG ideal model $I(r) = C\,(r/\omega)^4 e^{-2r^2/\omega^2} + bg$, fit by Nelder--Mead least-squares
        \item comparison Gaussian fit (TEM00) centered at same detected null, for reference
        \item $R^2$ computed for both LG and Gaussian fits
    \end{itemize}

    \item extinction ratio (ER)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item ER $= (\text{null min intensity} - bg) / (\text{ring peak intensity} - bg)$
        \item ring peak: mean of top 0.5\% of pixels (robust to hot pixels)
        \item null region: small window at detected center
    \end{itemize}

    \item beam size from radial profile
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $r_{peak}$: radius of peak azimuthally-averaged intensity
        \item inner/outer $1/e^2$ radii: interpolated crossing points on either side of the ring peak
    \end{itemize}

    \item ring symmetry (azimuthal analysis)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item intensity sampled at fixed radius over 360°, averaged over a small radial window
        \item coefficient of variation (uniformity of ring intensity)
        \item Fourier decomposition of azimuthal profile: $a_1$ (dipole/off-center), $a_2$ (elliptical distortion), $a_4$ (4-fold distortion)
        \item ring ellipticity: $\max(r_x,r_y)/\min(r_x,r_y)$
    \end{itemize}

    \item deflection sweep (range angle)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item voltage sweep per axis (X, Y), fixed ROI crop around donut center for each step
        \item center detected independently per frame; displacement relative to control center
        \item ER recomputed per step (same null/ring-peak method, on cropped ROI)
        \item NCC computed against the control ROI via 2D normalized cross-correlation
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
